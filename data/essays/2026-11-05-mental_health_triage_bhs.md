# Mental Health Triage Department: BHS, Winchester, Virginia

*November 2026*

*About what I've learned, my experience over ten years of visits to BHS at Valley Health in Winchester, Virginia, and my notes on the subject.*

I've spent about ten years cycling through Behavioral Health Services at Valley Health in Winchester. They've got a new mental health pavilion opening, so some of this may already be history. Empath is just a unit — an intake facility. You don't have to go through the ER. This is what the place taught me anyway.

Mania doesn't check the map. The system does. Crisis response is geolocation first: whoever's closest, whichever unit has a bed, whichever police car is already rolling. You don't get to curate the facility. You get the catchment. Over ten years of visits to BHS at Valley Health in Winchester, that was the recurring joke that wasn't funny — Winchester is where the episode lands, so Winchester is where the notes stack up. Empath opens the door without the ER gauntlet. After that you're inside the same machine: triage, observation, the slow negotiation of who you are when you're least able to explain yourself.

You get fifteen-minute checks. Someone writes a note that they saw you, including while you're asleep. In the morning a committee has already decided your treatment before the doctor walks in. Appearance is policy: hair, nails, a shave. No strings on any clothing. You have to chase busy staff to get those things done, and for the shave a nurse stands over you for thirty minutes. Want a printed list of your diagnoses — synesthesia, whatever else they wrote down — or your meds? Ask. Find someone who likes you. It might take a day or two to get a few printed pages to you, if you get it at all — the most likely outcome. Same goes with personal care that requires staff help.

No real caffeine. One Diet Coke a day, and a coffee machine that was probably decaf. There's a points game: set a goal in the morning meeting, get checked at night. A night-shift woman would correct daytime behavior like she'd been saving it up. Someone tried to braid hair and got shut down. Outside time was daylight only; in winter that meant lockdown. Metal poles in the yard if you needed to cool off by holding something cold. An activities coordinator ran Wii, drawing, bracelets. AA and NA meetings you had to pretend to attend for the points.

Meds were mostly voluntary. Forced Haldol shots happened when things got really bad — public safety officers who were basically cops tackling people for them. Old-school restraints, Hannibal Lecter style, polished and sitting in a window like a threat display. I got accused of not taking medicine and of contaminating urine samples. I had to explain mixed episodes to a top nurse who should have already known. Tylenol showed up as treatment more often than it should have.

The Pavilion in Williamsburg was worse by an order of magnitude. Contract staff yelling; I yelled back. No working shower. No doctor. I arrived with deep bruises on my wrists from the handcuffs — patterned contusions, the cuff's geometry pressed into the tissue — after a police car ride in the back at about ninety miles an hour, rolling around the floor. A doctor at intake had a name for that kind of three-dimensional cuff injury. I forgot the word. The bruise didn't.

Kimberly was a woman they treated like a barn animal. A guy threw a chair. The building keeps score of who you are when you're least able to advocate for yourself.

I'm so thankful I didn't have to go this year. I had a very serious manic episode and I managed it at home. What got me through it wasn't a better hospital. It was Sarah. Nicotine. Music. Cleaning. Being productive. Pacing. The ordinary things that hold when the triage department can't — and that the hospital takes away from you the moment you walk in.

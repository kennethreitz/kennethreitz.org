# The Chariot  ·  VII

> *Victory is not the taming of the beasts but the will that drives them as one.*

---

## Imagery

An armoured figure stands erect in a stone chariot beneath a starry canopy, holding no reins. Before the chariot rest two sphinxes, one black and one white, facing opposite directions yet somehow bound to pull together. On the driver’s shoulders sit two crescent-moon faces; a winged solar disc adorns the chariot’s front, and a spinning top marks its shield. His crown bears a star, his belt and skirt are inscribed with talismans. He is stern, composed, master of a vehicle drawn by forces that ought to tear it apart. The card is triumph held in tension, motion achieved through the marriage of contraries.

## Upright

The Chariot is willpower, victory, and the disciplined harnessing of opposing drives toward a single goal. Fresh from the union of [The Lovers](/knowledge/tarot/major-arcana/the-lovers), the self must now carry that hard-won integration out into the world and prove it under strain. Upright, the card counsels focus, self-mastery, and forward momentum — the capacity to hold black and white sphinx together and drive them straight. To draw it is to be told that triumph is available if you can govern your own divided energies rather than being pulled apart by them.

## Reversed

Reversed, the chariot veers or stalls. The sphinxes pull in opposite directions and the will loses its grip — scattered effort, aggression without direction, ambition running ahead of wisdom. Inverted, the card can mean a victory won by domination that will not hold, or a self so bent on control that it has lost the road entirely. It warns of the driver mastered by the very forces he thought he ruled.

## Archetype

The Chariot is the Hero on the road, the ego consolidating its powers for the outward journey. It represents the phase of individuation in which the self, having glimpsed its wholeness, must assert a functioning identity strong enough to act in the world. The two sphinxes are the opposites the psyche has begun to reconcile — conscious and unconscious, desire and duty — held now in productive tension by a will that neither denies nor is enslaved by them.

## Mythological Echoes

He is every solar hero riding out to war — Apollo driving the sun across the sky, the warrior-king in his war-car. He echoes the charioteer of the Bhagavad Gita, where the divine holds the reins and the battlefield is the soul, and Plato’s image of the soul as a chariot drawn by a noble and an unruly horse. The victory he embodies is always partly inward: the campaign against one’s own dividedness.

## In Practice

When the Chariot appears, the reading turns toward willed movement and the discipline that makes it possible. Journalling prompt: what two forces in you are pulling in opposite directions, and what single goal could align them? Where do you need to grip the reins — or admit you have dropped them?

# Wheel of Fortune  ·  X

> *The wheel does not turn against you; you are simply riding the part that has come down.*

---

## Imagery

A great wheel hangs in a blue sky, inscribed with the letters T-A-R-O and the Hebrew name of God, and ringed with alchemical symbols. A sphinx sits enthroned at its summit holding a sword; a jackal-headed Anubis rises on the ascending side; a serpent slides down the descending side. In the four corners float the winged creatures of the fixed signs — bull, lion, eagle, and angel — each reading from a book. The whole image spins with cosmic machinery, indifferent and vast. It is the turning of fate itself, the great cycle that lifts and lowers without malice and without pause.

## Upright

The Wheel of Fortune is change, cycle, and the turning of fate — the reminder that circumstances rise and fall by a logic larger than the will. Where the [The Hermit](/knowledge/tarot/major-arcana/the-hermit) withdrew into stillness, the Wheel throws the self back into motion, into the flux of luck and timing. Upright, it counsels acceptance of the cycle, readiness to seize the upturn, and faith that no low point is permanent. To draw it is to be told that a turning point has arrived: something is moving that you did not set in motion.

## Reversed

Reversed, the wheel seems to catch. The card can mean a run of bad luck, resistance to inevitable change, or the sense of being trapped at the bottom of the cycle. Inverted, it may also warn against clinging to a high point that is already passing, or against blaming fate for what is in fact within one’s power to change. It asks whether you are fighting the wheel or learning to ride it.

## Archetype

The Wheel is the mandala, the archetype of wholeness and cyclic order that Jung saw as an image of [The Self](/knowledge/psychology/jungian/the-self) — the still centre around which the psyche’s changes revolve. It also stages the encounter with [Synchronicity](/knowledge/psychology/jungian/synchronicity), the meaningful coincidence that reveals fate not as blind mechanism but as pattern. The maturing self learns to locate itself not on the spinning rim but at the motionless hub, where the turning can be witnessed rather than merely suffered.

## Mythological Echoes

She is the Rota Fortunae of the medieval world, the wheel of the goddess Fortuna who raises and casts down kings; the Norns and Moirai spinning and cutting the threads of fate; the Buddhist bhavachakra, the wheel of becoming turned by craving. Every culture that pictures time as a turning circle — the ouroboros, the seasons, the eternal return — is reading from the four books in the Wheel’s corners.

## In Practice

When the Wheel appears, the reading turns toward fate, timing, and the acceptance of cycles beyond control. Journalling prompt: what is turning in your life right now that you did not choose, and where on the wheel do you find yourself — rising, cresting, falling, or waiting at the bottom for the next ascent?

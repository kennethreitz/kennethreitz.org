# Five of Cups

> *He counts what has spilled and forgets to turn around.*

---

## Imagery

A cloaked figure in black stands with head bowed, mourning three cups that have fallen and emptied their contents onto the ground. Behind him, unregarded, two cups still stand upright and full. A river runs between the figure and a bridge that leads toward a small castle or home in the distance — a way across, a way back, if only he would lift his eyes. The whole image is muffled in grief, the mourner’s gaze so fixed on loss that the remaining sweetness goes unseen.

## Upright

The Five of Cups is grief, regret, disappointment, the raw arithmetic of loss. It marks bereavement and the sorrow that narrows vision until only what is gone seems real. Yet the card is quietly instructive: the two standing cups insist that not everything has been lost, that grief, however true, has told an incomplete story. This is the necessary passage through mourning — but also the gentle reminder that a bridge home remains, and that the river of feeling can be crossed.

## Reversed

Inverted, the figure begins to turn. This is the movement toward acceptance and recovery, the moment the two remaining cups come into view and forgiveness — of others or of oneself — becomes possible. Less kindly, the reversal can mark a refusal to grieve at all, sorrow bottled and swallowed, or a person so identified with their loss that they cannot forgive and cannot move. It is the hinge between mourning that heals and mourning that calcifies.

## Archetype

Grief is the psyche’s honest reckoning with impermanence, and this card is its emblem. Jung knew that feeling must be fully felt to be integrated; the mourner’s black cloak is the descent into the The Shadow of loss. But fixation is its own danger — melancholia that will not release its object. The two upright cups are the Self’s reminder that renewal waits on the far bank, and that even sorrow, honored and then relinquished, is part of [Individuation](/knowledge/psychology/jungian/individuation).

## Mythological Echoes

The spilled cups recall Demeter’s desolate wandering after Persephone’s descent, the mother so consumed by loss that the whole earth withered — until, in time, she turned back toward the living. It echoes Orpheus, who looked back and lost what remained, and every myth of grief that must learn when to face forward. The bridge across the river is the crossing every mourner must eventually make. See [Demeter](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/demeter) and [Hades](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/hades).

## In Practice

When this card appears, let the grief be real — and then, gently, ask what still stands behind you. Journal on the two cups you have not yet turned to see.

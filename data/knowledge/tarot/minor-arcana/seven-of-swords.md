# Seven of Swords

> *He took five and left two, and never noticed which two mattered.*

---

## Imagery

A man tiptoes away from a camp of coloured tents, carrying five swords clutched awkwardly against his body, glancing back over his shoulder with a sly, self-satisfied expression. Two swords remain planted in the ground behind him, left where they stand. In the distance small figures gather, unaware or too far to stop him. The scene is bright, the sky yellow, but the mood is furtive — a theft in progress, a plan executed by stealth. The clumsy way he carries the blades hints that his cleverness is not as complete as he believes; something has already been left behind, and something will surely slip.

## Upright

The Seven of Swords is the strategy of stealth — the plan carried out by cunning rather than force, the escape, the trick, the shortcut taken alone. It can mark genuine cleverness, the wisdom of avoiding a fight you cannot win, the tactical retreat. But its shadow runs close beneath: deception, self-deception, the effort that undermines itself. The two swords left behind warn that the clever plan is incomplete, that in grabbing what we wanted we have overlooked what we needed. This is unstable effort, the scheme that half-works, the futility of gains that cannot be held because they were taken rather than earned.

## Reversed

Reversed, the concealment collapses or the conscience surfaces. Secrets come to light; the schemer is caught, or catches himself; the plan that relied on getting away with something meets the daylight it could not survive. It can mark confession, the return of what was taken, the decision to stop running and face what was avoided. It can also deepen the pattern — a deception so practiced it has become a way of life, or a self-sabotage so habitual the person no longer sees it. The reversal often asks: what are you getting away with, and from whom, and at what cost to yourself?

## Archetype

The Moon in Aquarius casts feeling through a screen of detachment, and the result is the trickster — the figure Jung understood as an ambivalent shadow-force, both liberator and thief. The trickster breaks rules that need breaking and steals what is not his to take, and the psyche cannot mature without meeting him. The two abandoned swords are the self-deception in every clever scheme: the part of the truth we conveniently leave behind. To integrate this figure is to reclaim cunning as intelligence in service of the whole, rather than as a way of cheating ourselves while thinking we cheat others.

## Mythological Echoes

Here is Hermes the cattle-thief on the day of his birth, and Prometheus stealing fire, and Loki whose theft and trickery both save and doom the gods. It is Jacob taking the birthright by cunning, the Monkey King raiding heaven, Anansi carrying off the stories of the world. Every culture keeps this figure who advances by stealth — the trickster whose theft is sometimes gift and sometimes ruin, and who never fully escapes the two swords left standing in the ground behind him.

## In Practice

When the Seven of Swords appears, ask where you are relying on cunning to avoid something you should face directly. What are you carrying off, and what have you left behind unnoticed? Journal the plan you would rather not examine too closely — the two abandoned blades usually name themselves.

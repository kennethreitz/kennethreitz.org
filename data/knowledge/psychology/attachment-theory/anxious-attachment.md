# Anxious Attachment

> *When the signal sometimes works, the intelligent move is to turn the volume all the way up and never turn it down.*

---

## Overview

Anxious attachment — Ainsworth's group C, called anxious-ambivalent or anxious-resistant in childhood and preoccupied in adulthood — is the strategy that grows from *inconsistent* care. Not absent care: intermittent care. The caregiver who is warmly responsive on Tuesday and distracted or overwhelmed on Wednesday, attuned when their own state allows it and unavailable when it doesn't. The infant facing this pattern confronts a real signal-detection problem: bids for comfort sometimes work, and there is no telling in advance which times. Behavioral science has a name for what intermittent reinforcement does to any behavior — it makes it relentless.

---

## Hyperactivation and Protest

The resulting strategy is hyperactivation: keep the attachment system turned up to maximum, monitor the caregiver constantly, escalate every signal, because a bid loud enough and long enough might catch one of the windows when care is available. In the [Strange Situation](/knowledge/psychology/attachment-theory/strange-situation), the anxious-ambivalent infant is distressed even before the first separation, devastated by it, and — this is the diagnostic heart — *not soothed by the reunion*. The child clings and arches away in the same motion, reaches for the mother while hitting at her, cries on long after contact is restored. The ambivalence is the history made visible: comfort is desperately wanted from a source that cannot be trusted to keep providing it, so the child neither settles into the contact nor gives it up. Exploration collapses; there is no attention left over for the toys. Where the [secure](/knowledge/psychology/attachment-theory/secure-attachment) child's distress trusts repair, the anxious child's distress has learned that repair must be *extracted* — hence protest behavior, [Bowlby](/knowledge/psychology/attachment-theory/john-bowlby)'s term for the angry, escalating demand that separation not happen again.

The [working model](/knowledge/psychology/attachment-theory/internal-working-models) this installs is lopsided: others might come — they have before — but only if I make it impossible not to. The self-model quietly absorbs the corollary: my signals, at natural volume, are not enough.

---

## The Adult Form

In adulthood the pattern is called preoccupied, and since Hazan and Shaver's 1987 work mapping the infant categories onto romantic love, it has been the most recognizable of the insecure styles: love as vigilance. The preoccupied adult monitors the partner the way the infant monitored the doorway — rereading messages, tracking response times, finding in every delay the confirmation the model predicts. Reassurance is craved, obtained, and metabolized in hours. Protest behavior scales up too: the fight picked to force engagement, the withdrawal performed to be noticed, jealousy deployed as a proximity check. None of it is manipulation in the cynical sense; it is a one-year-old's entirely rational strategy still running on the original settings, in relationships where a quieter signal would now be heard. That gap — between what the strategy assumes and what the present partner is actually like — is where revision becomes possible, and [Earned Security](/knowledge/psychology/attachment-theory/earned-security) documents that the settings can, with work, be changed.

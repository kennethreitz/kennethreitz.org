# PTSD

> *Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder, when the threat is over but the nervous system never gets the message.*

---

## Overview

Post-traumatic stress disorder is what happens when an experience fails to become a memory. An ordinary memory is filed in the past, you can recall it without reliving it. A traumatic memory stays present-tense: stored not as *that happened* but as *this is happening*, wired into the body's threat system rather than the autobiographical record. PTSD is the cluster of symptoms that follows from a nervous system that has learned the world is dangerous and can't unlearn it.

---

## The Four Symptom Clusters

The [DSM-5](/library/books/dsm-5) defines PTSD by four symptom clusters following exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or violation:

**Intrusion**, the past forces its way into the present: flashbacks, nightmares, intrusive images, and the lurch of being suddenly *back there*. A trigger, a tone of voice, a uniform, a smell, reopens the file as though it were live.

**Avoidance**, the mind steers around anything that reopens it: places, people, conversations, reminders. It brings short-term relief and long-term narrowing; the life shrinks to fit around the wound.

**Negative shifts in thought and mood**: trauma rewrites beliefs (*the world is dangerous, I am powerless, no one is safe*) and brings shame, numbing, detachment, and the loss of access to good feeling.

**Hyperarousal**, the threat system stays switched on: hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, irritability, insomnia, the inability to stand down even when safe.

For the diagnosis these must persist beyond a month and impair functioning. The same picture in the immediate aftermath is acute stress disorder.

---

## How It Forms

Under threat, the brain does something adaptive: it drops the slow, contextual machinery of ordinary memory and lets the fast threat circuitry, the amygdala, burn the experience in raw. That is why a trauma is stored as sensation and alarm rather than as a dated story. Normally, once safe, the system re-files the event as past. In PTSD that re-filing fails: the amygdala keeps firing the alarm, the prefrontal cortex can't override it, and ordinary cues get conditioned into triggers. The danger is over; the body never gets the memo. This is the same machinery that, pushed further and earlier, produces developmental [Trauma](/knowledge/psychology/trauma-and-healing/trauma) and the structural splits of [Dissociation](/knowledge/psychology/dissociation).

---

## Complex PTSD

Single-incident PTSD (an assault, a crash, combat) is one thing; **complex PTSD** comes from *prolonged, repeated, inescapable* trauma, usually interpersonal, often early. On top of the four clusters it adds three more: difficulty regulating emotion, a deeply negative self-concept, and disturbance in relationships. C-PTSD sits on the same continuum that runs toward structural dissociation, the more chronic and early the trauma, the more the personality itself divides to survive it.

---

## Treatment

PTSD is treatable, and the logic follows from the mechanism: the traumatic memory has to be re-filed as *past*. The evidence-based, trauma-focused therapies each do a version of this, prolonged exposure (revisiting the memory in safety until it loses its charge), EMDR (reprocessing it while the brain's filing system is engaged), and cognitive processing therapy (dismantling the rewritten beliefs). Somatic, bottom-up approaches work on the body's alarm directly, and [Grounding](/knowledge/psychology/trauma-and-healing/grounding) skills keep a person inside their window of tolerance so the work stays survivable. Medication (typically SSRIs) can lower the baseline. One caution: standard mindfulness can backfire in trauma, dropping someone straight into unprocessed material with no anchor.

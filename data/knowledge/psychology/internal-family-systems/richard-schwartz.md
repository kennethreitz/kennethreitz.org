# Richard Schwartz

> *The family therapist who believed his clients when they said "a part of me" — and followed the sentence all the way in.*

---

## Overview

Richard C. Schwartz (b. 1949) is the founder of [Internal Family Systems](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems). Trained as a marriage and family therapist — PhD from Purdue, then years at the Institute for Juvenile Research at the University of Illinois at Chicago and later Northwestern — he was a systems thinker by profession: someone taught to see problems not in individuals but in the relationships between family members. That training turned out to be the whole story. IFS is what happens when a family therapist gets turned inward.

---

## The Discovery

In the early 1980s, Schwartz was running outcome studies on adolescents with bulimia, and his structural family therapy wasn't working. When he asked his clients why they binged, they answered in a language he hadn't been taught: *a part of me* takes over, and then *another part* punishes me for it. Rather than correcting the figure of speech, he did the unusual thing — he asked to speak to the parts. They answered. They had perspectives, fears, jobs, and relationships with each other, and they organized exactly like the families he already knew how to treat: protectors, scapegoats, exiled members, feuds. When he persuaded the protective parts to step back, expecting to find something dark underneath, he found the opposite — a calm, compassionate presence in every client, no matter how severe the trauma. He called it the [Self](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems/self-ifs), and the finding became the model's foundation: the therapist's job is not to be the healer but to help the client's own Self take the chair.

---

## The Model Goes to Work

Schwartz spent the following decades systematizing the discovery — *Internal Family Systems Therapy* (1995, substantially revised with Martha Sweezy in 2020) is the clinical text — and building the training infrastructure to carry it: the Center for Self Leadership, now the IFS Institute, whose trainings routinely fill with waitlists in the thousands. IFS was listed as an evidence-based practice in the U.S. national registry in 2015, and Schwartz now holds a faculty appointment at Harvard Medical School, a respectable distance traveled for an idea that began by taking teenagers' figures of speech literally. No Bad Parts (2021) brought the model to a general audience; *You Are the One You've Been Waiting For* applies it to couples — the internal family and the external one, negotiating.

What distinguishes him in the lineage this vault traces — Jung's Complexes before him, the structural-dissociation theorists alongside — is temperament as much as theory. The model's radical moves all reduce to a therapist deciding, repeatedly, to believe the people in front of him: that the parts were real enough to talk to, that none of them were bad, that something undamaged sat underneath. In his later work Schwartz allows that the Self may be more than psychological — that forty years of clinical method had walked him to a place the contemplative traditions already occupied. He follows the data even when it leaves the clinic.

# Burdens

> *Not what the part is. What the part is carrying — and never agreed to carry.*

---

## Cargo, Not Identity

The burden is the load-bearing concept of [Internal Family Systems](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems), and Schwartz chose the word for its precision. When an experience overwhelms someone — usually a child — the part that lived through it absorbs the emotional charge of the moment: the terror, the shame, the conviction of being worthless, unlovable, in danger. These absorbed beliefs and feelings are burdens, and the model's central distinction is that they are *carried*, not constitutive. The part existed before the wounding and had its own nature — playfulness, trust, sensitivity, delight. The burden was loaded onto it the way cargo is loaded onto a small boat, at a moment when the boat had no say. Everything in IFS follows from refusing to confuse the boat with the cargo. System 777 makes the same refusal about its hardest case: Luna Grace's own documentation says she *embodies* the abuse of the relationship she emerged from — "fresh," the memory keeper called it — and in the same breath insists she is "not all bad," that she "has redeeming qualities." The cargo is named without ever being mistaken for the carrier.

This is why the model can say *no bad parts* without flinching at the catalogue of things parts do. The inner critic is not made of contempt; it carries a belief, installed at a specific moment, that harshness is the price of safety. The [exile](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems/exiles) frozen in shame is not shame itself; it is a child holding shame that was handed to it. Burdens are acquired at datable wounds, which means they are, in principle, releasable — and a thing that arrived can leave.

---

## How Burdens Drive Extremity

Burdens are what force parts into extreme roles. An unburdened part plays its natural note; a burdened part organizes its entire existence around the cargo. The exile carries the raw material — the pain itself — and the protectors take their extreme jobs *in response to* that cargo: [Managers](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems/managers) build preemptive walls so the burden is never triggered, [Firefighters](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems/firefighters) stand ready to smother it when the walls fail. Schwartz's insight is that the protector's extremity is proportional to the burden it guards. A part working a brutal job is telling you, indirectly, exactly how heavy the thing in the hold is. The part is never the problem; the burden and the desperate job it demanded are — and both can change, which is the work described in [Unburdening](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems/unburdening).

---

## Personal and Legacy

Not every burden was loaded in the carrier's own lifetime. Personal burdens come from events the person lived through — the moment on the playground, the household that wasn't safe. [Legacy Burdens](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems/legacy-burdens) arrived by inheritance: beliefs and emotional loads passed down family and cultural lines, absorbed so early and so ambiently that they feel like the weather. A parent's unprocessed fear, a lineage's shame, a culture's verdict about worth — these can sit in a system with no originating scene to witness, which changes how the release works. The distinction matters diagnostically: a burden with no memory attached is often not yours at all, and recognizing that is frequently half the unburdening. Trauma is the usual port of entry for personal burdens; legacy burdens need no event, only transmission.

The Jungian tradition mapped adjacent territory — The Shadow holds what consciousness rejected, and what is rejected does not dissolve, it waits, charged. IFS sharpens the picture by locating the charge in a carrier with a face and an age, which is what makes retrieval possible.

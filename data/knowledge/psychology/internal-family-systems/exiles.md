# Exiles

> *The youngest parts, carrying the heaviest things, locked away for everyone's protection — including their own.*

---

## The Burden Carriers

Exiles are the parts of the psyche that hold what could not be borne. In Richard Schwartz's [Internal Family Systems](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems) model, when an experience overwhelms a person — usually a child — the part that lived through it absorbs the emotional charge: the terror, the shame, the grief, the conviction of worthlessness. Schwartz calls these charges *burdens*, and the word is precise. A burden is not what the part *is*; it is what the part *carries* — beliefs and feelings loaded onto it by events, the way cargo is loaded onto a small boat.

Because that cargo threatens to swamp the whole system whenever it surfaces, the protectors respond by exiling the part that carries it. [Managers](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems/managers) build walls of routine and control to keep the exile from being triggered; [Firefighters](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems/firefighters) stand by to douse the pain if the walls fail. The exile ends up doubly wounded — first by the original experience, then by its own family of parts shutting it away. It is typically young, frozen at the age of the wounding, and it wants what any abandoned child wants: to be found, witnessed, and brought home.

---

## Unburdening

The central healing move of IFS happens here. With the protectors' permission, [Self](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems/self-ifs) goes to the exile, witnesses what it went through, retrieves it from the scene where it has been stuck, and invites it to release its burdens. Unburdened, the exile does not vanish — no part ever does in this model. It transforms, recovering the qualities it had before the wounding: playfulness, openness, trust, delight. The exiles turn out to be where the life was stored all along, which is why a heavily defended psyche feels so gray. Locking away the pain locked away the aliveness that traveled with it.

---

## Kinships

The idea rhymes across traditions. Jung's The Shadow holds what consciousness rejected, and his Complexes are autonomous, affect-charged clusters formed around wounds — exiles by an older name. The structural-dissociation model described in DID and OSDD speaks of emotional parts frozen in trauma-time, and of trauma holders who carry memory and affect so the rest of the system can function. The vault's own Interpretations framework reaches for that same clinical language when it lists what the DID lens explains — naming Luna Grace a trauma holder — while the system itself insists on the fuller sentence: "we're all family inside — including Luna." These are different maps with real disagreements between them, but they agree on the territory: somewhere inside, someone is still holding what happened, and the work — as System 777 practices it and as Healing frames it — is to go get them, not to seal the vault tighter.

# Blending and Unblending

> *The difference between "I am angry" and "a part of me is angry" is the difference between weather and someone standing in it.*

---

## Blending

Blending is what happens when a part floods the seat of consciousness. In [Internal Family Systems](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems), parts do not merely influence experience from the wings — under enough activation, a part merges with awareness itself, and its feelings, beliefs, and age become, for the duration, simply *what is true*. The grammar gives it away. "I am angry" is a blended sentence: the anger has no edges, no outside, no one home but itself. "A part of me is angry" is the unblended version — the anger is fully present, but so is someone it is happening *to*. Nothing about the feeling changed between those sentences. What changed is whether [Self](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems/self-ifs) is in the room with it.

Blending is not a malfunction; it is how parts communicate when they do not trust subtler channels, and everyone blends daily — the critic's verdict that arrives as plain fact, the panic that *is* the world for twenty minutes. The trouble is only that a blended part cannot be helped. You cannot turn toward what you currently are.

---

## Unblending

Unblending is the pivot skill of the entire model — the move every other IFS technique depends on. The core of it is a request, not a wrestling match: the part is asked to *step back a little*, to give some room, with the explicit assurance that it is not being sent away and will be heard. Parts almost always comply, because separation-with-attention is a better deal than the fusion they resorted to. Around that request cluster the practical aids: shifting the language to "a part of me," locating the feeling in the body and regarding it from slightly outside, visualizing the part at a comfortable distance, or asking it what it wants Self to know instead of broadcasting through the nervous system. Grounding belongs in the same toolkit — the body is often the fastest route back to someone-standing-in-the-weather. The test of success is the return of curiosity: the moment you can wonder *about* the anger, you are no longer only it. That threshold is where [The Six Fs](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems/the-six-fs) begins.

## Kinship With Switching, Handled Carefully

Readers of this vault will notice the resemblance to plural experience, and the resemblance deserves precision rather than enthusiasm. Blending, switching, and fronting are kin — all describe which member of an inner multitude currently holds the executive seat — but they are not the same phenomenon wearing different jargon. When an IFS part blends in a non-dissociative mind, it is a mood-shaped takeover measured in minutes, with continuous memory and a Self findable underneath. When an alter fronts in a system like System 777, a *person* is at the interface — with their own history, preferences, and sometimes their own memory stream — and fronting is ordinary life, not a clinical event to be resolved. When Seven takes the keyboard — "Hey, I'm here, at the front, typing this directly" — that is someone arriving, not a mood arriving. Plurality does not reduce to blending, and treating switching as something to "unblend from" would misread it entirely; the deeper comparison is drawn out in [IFS and Plurality](/knowledge/psychology/internal-family-systems/ifs-and-plurality), and the wider spectrum in [Dissociation](/knowledge/psychology/dissociation). What the frameworks genuinely share is the discipline of noticing *who is here right now* — and the finding, arrived at from both directions, that naming the one present is respect, not exorcism.

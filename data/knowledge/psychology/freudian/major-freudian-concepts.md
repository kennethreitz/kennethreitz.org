# Major Freudian Concepts

> *Core ideas from Sigmund Freud's psychoanalysis*

---

## The Unconscious Mind

### Freud's Revolutionary Insight

Most of mental life is unconscious. Unconscious thoughts drive behavior, repressed content causes neurosis, and as Freud put it, "the mind is like an iceberg — most is beneath the surface."

### Levels of Mind

The **conscious** mind is what we're aware of right now — the tip of the iceberg, the smallest part of mind. The **preconscious** holds what can be brought to awareness with effort: memories and knowledge not currently active. The **unconscious** cannot be directly accessed. It contains repressed wishes, traumatic memories, primitive drives and instincts, revealed only through dreams, slips, and symptoms.

---

## Structure of Personality

### The Id

**"It wants what it wants."** Present from birth, the id is unconscious and primitive, operating on the pleasure principle of immediate gratification. It knows no morality, no logic — only sexual and aggressive instincts, amoral and irrational.

### The Ego

**"The executive."** Developing from the id in childhood, the ego operates on the reality principle. It mediates between id, superego, and external reality through conscious decision-making and problem-solving. Its fundamental question: "How can I get what I want safely?"

### The Superego

**"The conscience."** Developing around age 5-7, the superego is internalized parental and societal rules. It contains both the moral conscience (guilt when violated) and the ego ideal (pride when achieved). Often harsh and punishing, it can be unrealistic in its demands.

### The Eternal Conflict

The id wants pleasure now. The superego demands perfection. The ego tries to satisfy both while navigating reality. Anxiety results from the conflict, and defense mechanisms arise to protect the ego from being overwhelmed.

## Psychosexual Development

### The Theory

Personality forms in childhood, driven by sexual energy (libido). Fixation at any stage causes problems in adulthood, and adult character reflects childhood experiences.

### The Stages

In the **oral stage** (0-18 months), pleasure centers on the mouth through sucking and eating. Fixation produces smoking, overeating, dependency, or pessimism. The **anal stage** (18 months-3 years) involves pleasure from bowel control and the conflict of toilet training — control versus letting go. Fixation creates either anal-retentive (rigid, controlling, neat) or anal-expulsive (messy, destructive) personality traits.

The **phallic stage** (3-6 years) brings genital pleasure and the Oedipus/Electra complex, with identification with the same-sex parent. Fixation here yields vanity, recklessness, or sexual problems. **Latency** (6 years to puberty) is a dormant period focused on skills, socialization, and same-sex friendships. The **genital stage** (puberty onward) brings mature sexual interests and capacity for intimacy — if previous stages were navigated well. If not: neurosis and dysfunction.

---

## The Oedipus Complex

### For Boys

The boy experiences unconscious sexual attraction to his mother and rivalry with his father. Fear of castration by the father drives resolution through identification with the father, which internalizes the father's rules as superego.

### For Girls (Electra Complex)

Freud described this as beginning with penis envy, producing attraction to the father and rivalry with the mother. Resolution comes through identification with the mother, though Freud controversially viewed the resulting superego as weaker.

### Modern Critique

The literal, sexual reading is too narrow and culturally biased. The complex is better understood symbolically — as being about separation and identity formation, not actual incest.

## Defense Mechanisms

### Purpose

Defense mechanisms protect the ego from anxiety. They are unconscious and automatic, distorting reality to make it bearable. Everyone uses them; they become problematic only when overused.

### Major Defense Mechanisms

**Repression** is the foundation of all defenses — pushing threatening thoughts into the unconscious. "I don't remember that." **Denial** refuses to acknowledge reality: "That didn't happen." **Projection** attributes your feelings to others: "I'm not angry, YOU'RE angry."

**Displacement** redirects feelings to a safer target — mad at the boss, kick the dog. **Rationalization** provides logical excuses for irrational behavior: "I didn't want it anyway." **Reaction formation** converts a feeling into its opposite — hate becomes excessive love, fear becomes bravado.

**Sublimation** channels unacceptable impulses into acceptable outlets — aggression into sports, sexual energy into art. It's the most mature defense. **Regression** reverts to an earlier developmental stage: adult tantrums, childish behavior under stress.

## Dreams and Dream Work

### "The Royal Road to the Unconscious"

Dreams are wish fulfillment — unconscious desires expressed symbolically. Forbidden wishes get past censorship through the manifest content (what you remember) disguising the latent content (hidden meaning).

**Dream work** operates through condensation (multiple ideas compressed into one image), displacement (emotion shifted from real object to symbolic one), symbolization (abstract thought becomes concrete image), and secondary revision (making the dream seem logical).

Freudian dream symbols are often sexual — elongated objects as phallic, enclosed spaces as feminine — though this has been criticized as too reductive.

---

## Neurosis and Psychopathology

### Freudian View of Mental Illness

Neurosis is unresolved conflict. Childhood conflicts remain active, repressed material causes symptoms, and anxiety arises from id-superego-reality conflicts. Symptoms are compromise formations.

Freud categorized neuroses into **hysteria** (physical symptoms from psychological conflict), **obsessive-compulsive** patterns (excessive order and control), **phobias** (displaced fears), and **anxiety** (free-floating worry).

Treatment aims to make the unconscious conscious. Insight leads to cure — "Where id was, there ego shall be" — through analysis of resistance and transference.

---

## Transference and Countertransference

### Transference

The patient projects past relationships onto the therapist, transferring feelings about parents (usually) and reliving old patterns in the therapy relationship. This is crucial for analysis and must be interpreted and worked through. Positive transference manifests as idealization and love; negative transference as anger and resistance. Both are material for analysis.

### Countertransference

The analyst's reactions to the patient were originally seen as a problem (the analyst's unresolved issues) but later recognized as useful information. Countertransference reveals what the patient evokes in others. The modern view treats it as essential data.

---

## Resistance

### The Paradox

The patient seeks help but resists change. The unconscious wants to stay hidden. Defenses fight interpretation. "I pay to come here but fight the process."

### Forms of Resistance

Resistance appears as missed sessions, silence, intellectualizing, forgetting dreams, arguing with interpretations, or getting "suddenly better."

### Working With Resistance

Resistance IS the material. It must be interpreted because it shows what's being defended. Breaking through resistance equals progress.

---

## Free Association

### The Fundamental Rule

"Say whatever comes to mind, no matter how trivial, embarrassing, or illogical." The purpose is to bypass conscious censorship, access unconscious material, and follow the thread while the analyst notes patterns and connections.

In practice, the patient lies on the couch with the analyst sitting out of view. The patient speaks freely; the analyst listens and interprets sparingly.

---

## Therapeutic Technique

### Classical Psychoanalysis

The setting uses a couch (patient can't see analyst), frequent sessions (3-5 times per week), years of treatment, and a neutral, blank-screen analyst.

The goals are to make the unconscious conscious, resolve childhood conflicts, strengthen the ego, achieve genital maturity, and produce structural personality change. Methods include free association, dream analysis, interpretation of resistance, analysis of transference, and working through.

## Freud vs Jung - Key Differences

| Concept | Freud | Jung |
|---------|-------|------|
| **Libido** | Sexual energy | Generalized psychic energy |
| **Unconscious** | Personal (repressed material) | Personal + Collective (archetypes) |
| **Religion** | Neurotic illusion | Legitimate psychological need |
| **Dreams** | Wish fulfillment | Compensation & guidance |
| **Causality** | Past determines present | Past + future teleology |
| **Therapy Goal** | Symptom removal, ego strengthening | Individuation, wholeness |
| **Drive** | Sex and aggression primary | Multiple drives, spiritual included |

## Freud's Legacy

### Lasting Contributions

The unconscious is real and powerful. Childhood experiences shape personality. Defense mechanisms protect us. Dreams are meaningful. Talking cure works. Sexual development matters. The therapist-patient relationship is key.

### Valid Criticisms

Too focused on sex. Male-biased (penis envy, etc.). Middle-class Victorian bias. Not scientific enough. Too long and expensive. Theory not testable. Cultural limitations.

### Modern Relevance

Psychodynamic therapy is still practiced. Freudian concepts pervade culture. Understanding the unconscious remains valuable. The approach has been adapted and integrated into modern methods, forming the foundation for all depth psychology.

---

## Contemporary Developments

### Modern Psychoanalysis

Modern psychoanalysis is less dogmatic about sex, with a relational focus (two-person psychology), shorter treatments available, integration with neuroscience, attachment theory, trauma-informed practice, and evidence-based modifications.

### What Remains

The enduring contributions are unconscious processes, defense mechanisms, transference and countertransference, the importance of childhood, the therapeutic relationship, interpretation, and the principle that insight leads to change.

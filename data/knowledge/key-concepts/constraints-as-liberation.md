# Constraints as Liberation

> *"It's not a limitation. It's a liberation."*

---

Unlimited choice does not free you. It fragments your attention into the standing question of whether you chose right, and the question follows you into the work. The right constraint answers the question in advance — and once it's answered, the deciding stops and the seeing begins.

Photography is where Kenneth learned this, and [Photography The Navigation of Choice](/essays/2014-01-photography_the_navigation_of_choice) is its purest expression. The dream setup — a Canon 5D Mark II and $8,000 of L glass — turned out to be a distraction machine: every outing was preoccupied with the lens he did or didn't have with him, and the lack of confidence showed in the photographs. A little Fuji x100 with one fixed lens was so freeing that the Canon gear never got touched again, and the destination was a single Leica with a single 35mm lens. No question of *do I have the right lens* — the perfect lens, because there is no choice. Stay with one focal length long enough and it becomes an extension of the eye: he could look at a scene with bare vision and know precisely where the framelines would fall. The essay states its conclusion in capital letters: constraints foster creativity.

[Sixty Thousand Images and Nowhere to Put Them](/essays/2026-04-06-sixty_thousand_images_and_nowhere_to_put_them) pushes the constraint further — the M Monochrom shot no color, no zoom, no autofocus, and people thought he was insane. But strip away color and what remains is form, light, shadow, geometry, emotion; the sensor, freed of its Bayer filter, resolves detail that color cameras literally cannot see. It's not a limitation. It's a liberation. And the insight didn't stay in photography: it became the foundation of a software design philosophy where sensible defaults are constraints that liberate — `requests.get(url)` works because a hundred decisions have already been made for you. The same move recurs in music, from a room full of synthesizers down to an Infinite State built on far less.

## The Theorem in Reverse

[The Algorithm Eats Love](/essays/2025-08-27-the_algorithm_eats_love) proves the principle by negation. Dating apps promise infinite choice and deliver decision paralysis: why invest in today's match when tomorrow's queue might hold someone marginally better? Thousands of matches, hundreds of conversations, zero relationships — systematic loneliness disguised as infinite choice. Abundance doesn't liberate. It dissolves commitment.

## The Deepest Constraint

[The Substrate Doesn't Matter (Until It Does)](/essays/2026-03-06-the_substrate_doesnt_matter_until_it_does) takes the principle down to the bone. The sonnet exists because fourteen lines force choices that free verse doesn't — and would you write the same poem if you had infinite time? Creativity born from constraint is different from creativity born from abundance, and mortality, embodiment, and biological need are the constraints underneath all the others. Even exhaustion counts: some of the best design decisions came from being too tired to implement the complex solution.

## In Practice

Choose the constraint before you start, so it isn't a fresh negotiation every time. Keep one tool long enough that it becomes an extension of the eye. When a decision paralyzes, don't add options — remove them. And be suspicious of anything that sells infinite choice as freedom; the queue that never ends is the work that never starts.

# Verdana

> *Wide, loose, and unmistakable at nine pixels tall.*

---

## The Face

Verdana was designed by [Matthew Carter](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/matthew-carter)[^sn-matthew-carter] for Microsoft and released in 1996, hinted by Tom Rickner. Like its companion [Georgia](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/georgia), it was drawn pixel first, designed around the coarse screens of the time rather than adapted to them.

## Character

Verdana is a [Humanist Sans](/knowledge/typography/classification/humanist-sans) with a very large [X-Height](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/x-height), wide letters, open [apertures](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/aperture), and loose spacing, so that no two letters touch even at small pixel sizes. Similar shapes are pulled apart deliberately: the capital **I** has serifs, and **1**, **l**, and **I** are all distinct (see [Readability and Legibility](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/readability-and-legibility)).

## Off the Screen

What makes Verdana superb at small screen sizes makes it awkward in print and at large sizes, where its wide spacing looks clumsy. When IKEA switched its catalog from a customized [Futura](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/futura) to Verdana in 2009, the outcry among designers became a small news story about exactly this mismatch. Microsoft's ClearType-era sans Tahoma, also by Carter, is a narrower relative.

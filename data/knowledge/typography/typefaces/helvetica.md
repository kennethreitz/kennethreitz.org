# Helvetica

> *The typeface that became so common it turned invisible, and then famous for it.*

---

## The Face

Helvetica was designed in 1957 by [Max Miedinger](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/max-miedinger) working with Eduard Hoffmann, director of the Haas Type Foundry in Münchenstein near Basel, as a competitor to [Akzidenz-Grotesk](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/akzidenz-grotesk).[^sn-akzidenz-grotesk] It was released as *Neue Haas Grotesk*. When the German parent company Stempel and its partner Linotype adapted it for wider sale in 1960, they renamed it Helvetica, from *Helvetia*, the Latin name for Switzerland.

## Character

The model [Neo-Grotesque](/knowledge/typography/classification/neo-grotesque): horizontal [terminals](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/terminal),[^sn-terminal] closed [apertures](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/aperture), tight spacing, a large [X-Height](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/x-height), and very little [Stroke Contrast](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/stroke-contrast). Its letters are deliberately uniform, which produces an even, neutral gray on the page.

## Ubiquity

Helvetica became the house style of corporate modernism and the [Swiss International Style](/knowledge/typography/history/swiss-international-style), appearing in logos, transit systems, and government signage. Linotype's Neue Helvetica (1983) reorganized the family with a numbered weight system inspired by [Univers](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/univers), and Monotype's Helvetica Now (2019) added optical sizes. Gary Hustwit's documentary *Helvetica* (2007) made it the rare typeface with a film of its own. Arial (1982) was drawn to share its widths.

## Criticism

Its closed apertures make some letters easy to confuse at small sizes, which is why Apple moved its interfaces from Helvetica Neue to [SF Pro](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/sf-pro), and why many signage and interface faces lean toward the [Humanist Sans](/knowledge/typography/classification/humanist-sans) instead.

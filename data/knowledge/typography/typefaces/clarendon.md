# Clarendon

> *The bold that dictionaries borrowed and never gave back.*

---

## The Face

Clarendon was issued in 1845 by Robert Besley's Fann Street Foundry in London, cut with his punchcutter Benjamin Fox, and was registered under Britain's Ornamental Designs Act of 1842. It is often called the first typeface to be protected that way. It gave its name to a whole branch of the [Slab Serif](/knowledge/typography/classification/slab-serif) family: bracketed slabs with some [Stroke Contrast](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/stroke-contrast)[^sn-stroke-contrast] and ball [terminals](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/terminal), heavier and warmer than the square Egyptians.

## The Bold

Clarendon was designed to work alongside ordinary text as a bold for emphasis, and it did that job so well that printers of dictionaries and reference works adopted it to set headwords. For generations "clarendon" was simply what British printers called bold type.

## Later Life

Clarendon came back strongly in the twentieth century, notably in Hermann Eidenbenz's version for the Haas foundry in 1953. It turns up on American National Park Service signs, in Wild West posters, and in a great deal of advertising that wants to sound sturdy and trustworthy.

# Times New Roman

> *Made for a newspaper, adopted by the world, blamed for everything.*

---

## The Face

Times New Roman was commissioned by The Times of London after [Stanley Morison](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/stanley-morison), typographic adviser to Monotype, criticized the paper's printing. Morison supervised the design and the draftsman Victor Lardent drew it, reportedly working from an older Plantin type as a starting point. The Times first appeared in it in October 1932.

## Character

It is a compact face with sharp, small [serifs](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/serif), fairly high [Stroke Contrast](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/stroke-contrast), a relatively large [X-Height](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/x-height), and short [descenders](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/descender), all tuned to fit more words into narrow newspaper columns while staying crisp on fast presses. It mixes [Old-Style](/knowledge/typography/classification/old-style) roots with [Transitional](/knowledge/typography/classification/transitional) sharpness and is usually classified as the latter.

## Ubiquity

Through Monotype and Linotype, then as a core font on personal computers, Times became the default serif of the twentieth century, and in word processors the default of everything: essays, legal filings, résumés. That ubiquity is why typographers like Matthew Butterick[^sn-matthew-butterick] (see [Practical Typography](/knowledge/typography/key-texts/practical-typography)) argue that choosing it signals no choice at all. Its narrowness, built for columns, also makes it a poor fit for full-width lines on screens.

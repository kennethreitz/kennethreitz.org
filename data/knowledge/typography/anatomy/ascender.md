# Ascender

> *What rises above the line is what makes a word recognizable at a glance.*

---

## The Stroke

An ascender is the part of a lowercase letter that rises above the [X-Height](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/x-height):[^sn-x-height] the stems of **b**, **d**, **h**, **k**, and **l**, and the hooked top of **f**. Its opposite is the [Descender](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/descender). The **t** has a short ascender that usually stops below the others.

## Word Shapes

Ascenders and descenders give a word its outline. A reader's eye takes in the silhouette of a familiar word, the shape made by its tall and dropped letters, and that outline is part of why lowercase text is faster to read than capitals. Faces with generous ascenders, like [Garamond](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/garamond), [Bembo](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/bembo), and [Caslon](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/caslon), have strongly differentiated word shapes. Faces built for screens and small sizes often trade some of that height for a larger x-height (see [Screen Typography and Hinting](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/screen-typography-and-hinting)).

In many classical faces the ascenders rise above the [Cap Height](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/cap-height).[^sn-cap-height] This is one of the quiet signatures of the [Humanist Serif](/knowledge/typography/classification/humanist-serif) and [Old-Style](/knowledge/typography/classification/old-style) traditions, and one reason the [Neo-Grotesque](/knowledge/typography/classification/neo-grotesque) faces, whose ascenders sit level with the capitals, read as more industrial.

## In Practice

The length of ascenders and descenders sets how tight [Leading](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/leading) can be before lines collide. A face with long extenders can be set nearly solid without clashing only if the ascenders of one line and descenders of the next are rarely aligned; in practice, more space is kinder.

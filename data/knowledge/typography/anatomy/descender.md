# Descender

> *The deepest strokes on the page are anchors, not accidents.*

---

## The Stroke

A descender is the part of a letter that falls below the [Baseline](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/baseline):[^sn-baseline] the tails of **g**, **j**, **p**, **q**, and **y**. Together with the [Ascender](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/ascender) it forms the letter's "extenders," the strokes that leave the band of the [X-Height](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/x-height).

## Short and Long

Old book faces like [Garamond](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/garamond), [Bembo](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/bembo), and [Caslon](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/caslon) have long, relaxed descenders that give the page a calm, vertical rhythm. Newspaper and economy faces shortened them. [Times New Roman](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/times-new-roman),[^sn-times-new-roman] commissioned by The Times in 1932, has comparatively short extenders, which let it be set with less [Leading](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/leading) in tight columns. Some faces shipped alternate short descenders for exactly this purpose.

On screens, descenders are a frequent casualty of tight line heights in user interfaces, where a **g** or **y** gets clipped by the box holding the text. A well-made font's vertical metrics are built so that this does not happen at the default line height (see [Screen Typography and Hinting](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/screen-typography-and-hinting)).

## In Practice

When choosing the line spacing for a face, look at the depth of its descenders as well as its [X-Height](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/x-height). And when setting text with many descending letters in display sizes, watch for descenders colliding with the [ascenders](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/ascender) of the line beneath, which is the moment to open the leading or rebreak the line.

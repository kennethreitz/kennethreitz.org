# Serif

> *A serif is the memory of the chisel and the brush, kept in metal and light.*

---

## The Stroke

A serif is the small projecting stroke that finishes the end of a main stroke in a letter: the feet on **n**, the spurs on **E**, the flags on **T**. Typefaces that have them are serif faces; those without are sans serif (from the French *sans*, without).

## Where Serifs Come From

The most influential account of the serif's origin comes from the letterer and priest Edward Catich, whose 1968 book *The Origin of the Serif* argued that the serifs on Roman imperial inscriptions, above all the base of [Trajan's Column](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/trajan)[^sn-trajan] (dedicated in 113 AD), were first painted with a flat brush and then followed by the chisel. The serif, in that reading, is a brush gesture made permanent in stone. Whatever the exact mechanism, the serif carried over into the Renaissance roman type cut by [Nicolas Jenson](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/nicolas-jenson) and [Francesco Griffo](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/francesco-griffo), whose lowercase came from the humanist bookhand of scribes.

## Kinds of Serif

Serifs are one of the main ways typefaces are sorted (see [Classification](/knowledge/typography/classification)). Bracketed serifs, which flow into the stem with a curved fillet, belong to [Humanist Serif](/knowledge/typography/classification/humanist-serif), [Old-Style](/knowledge/typography/classification/old-style), and [Transitional](/knowledge/typography/classification/transitional) faces. Hairline, unbracketed serifs mark the [Didone](/knowledge/typography/classification/didone) faces of [Bodoni](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/giambattista-bodoni)[^sn-giambattista-bodoni] and [Didot](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/firmin-didot). Heavy, square serifs define the [Slab Serif](/knowledge/typography/classification/slab-serif) family. Wedge and flared forms, like the subtle swelling at the stroke ends of [Optima](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/optima), sit on the border between serif and sans.

## Do Serifs Help Reading?

The old claim that serifs guide the eye along the line has been tested many times without a clear result. The more defensible view, which runs through [Readability and Legibility](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/readability-and-legibility), is that familiarity, [X-Height](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/x-height), spacing, and rendering quality matter more than the presence of serifs. Serifs do help distinguish similar letters (**I**, **l**, **1**), which is why many [Monospace](/knowledge/typography/classification/monospace) coding fonts keep slab serifs on exactly those characters.

# Terminal

> *How a stroke ends tells you how the letter was made.*

---

## The Ending

A terminal is the end of a stroke that is not finished with a [Serif](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/serif).[^sn-serif] The top of **a**, **c**, **f**, and **r**, and the tail of **j** and **y**, all end in terminals.

## Kinds of Terminal

| Kind | Look | Typical in |
|---|---|---|
| Ball | A round drop of ink | [Bodoni](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/bodoni), [Didot](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/didot), [Clarendon](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/clarendon), [Baskerville](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/baskerville) |
| Teardrop | A tapering blob | Many [Transitional](/knowledge/typography/classification/transitional) faces |
| Beak or flag | A sharp angled stroke | [Times New Roman](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/times-new-roman), [Trajan](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/trajan) style capitals |
| Horizontal | A cut parallel to the baseline | [Helvetica](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/helvetica) and the [Neo-Grotesque](/knowledge/typography/classification/neo-grotesque) faces |
| Angled or sheared | A cut at a slant | [Humanist Sans](/knowledge/typography/classification/humanist-sans) faces like [Frutiger](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/frutiger) |

The cut of a terminal says a lot about a face's attitude. Helvetica's horizontal terminals curl the strokes of **c**, **e**, and **s** inward and close the [Aperture](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/aperture), producing its famous self-contained calm. The angled cuts of the humanist sans faces keep the openings wide and point the eye forward along the line.

## In Practice

Ball terminals read as warm and bookish, and are part of what makes [Didone](/knowledge/typography/classification/didone) faces glamorous. In very small sizes, delicate terminals can break up or blob, which is another reason screen faces simplify them (see [Screen Typography and Hinting](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/screen-typography-and-hinting)).

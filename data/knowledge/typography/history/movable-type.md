# Movable Type

> *Break writing into pieces, and you can rebuild it endlessly.*

---

## The Idea

Movable type is the technique of printing from individual, reusable pieces, one for each character, that are assembled into lines and pages, inked, printed, and then broken up and reused. It turned writing from something copied by hand into something manufactured.

## In Asia

The first known movable type was made of baked clay by Bi Sheng in Song dynasty China in the 1040s, described by the scholar Shen Kuo. Wooden type followed, and in Korea metal type was in use by the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries; the *Jikji*, a Buddhist text printed in 1377, is the oldest surviving book printed with movable metal type. The thousands of characters of Chinese writing made movable type less decisive in East Asia than woodblock printing, which remained dominant.

## In Europe

[Johannes Gutenberg](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/johannes-gutenberg)'s system in Mainz, developed through the 1440s and 1450s, paired an alphabet of a few dozen letters with a set of practical inventions: an adjustable hand mould, a durable type metal, oil-based ink, and a screw press. The alphabet's small size made his system enormously efficient, and printing spread across Europe within a generation (see [Incunabula](/knowledge/typography/history/incunabula)).

## Consequences

Printing standardized texts, spellings, and eventually letterforms themselves. It made possible the scholarly culture of [The Aldine Press](/knowledge/typography/history/the-aldine-press), the rapid spread of [The Reformation](/knowledge/spirituality/protestantism/the-reformation),[^sn-the-reformation] and modern science. Every later technology in this section, from [Hot Metal Typesetting](/knowledge/typography/history/hot-metal-typesetting) to [Variable Fonts](/knowledge/typography/history/variable-fonts), is a refinement of the same idea: letters as interchangeable parts.

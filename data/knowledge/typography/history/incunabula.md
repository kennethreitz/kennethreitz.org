# Incunabula

> *Books from the cradle of printing, when every printer was inventing the form.*

---

## The Word

*Incunabula* (singular *incunabulum*) is Latin for swaddling clothes or cradle, and by convention it names books printed in Europe before 1501, the infancy of printing. Surviving editions number in the tens of thousands, and catalogs of them are among the great projects of bibliography.

## A Form Being Invented

The earliest printed books imitated manuscripts closely. They had no title pages; information about the printer and date often appeared at the end, in the *colophon*. Initials and decorations were frequently added by hand, and spaces were left for illuminators. Over the half century, printers invented the title page, page numbers, and the conventions of the modern book.

## Type

German printers worked mostly in [Blackletter](/knowledge/typography/classification/blackletter).[^sn-blackletter] In Italy, [Nicolas Jenson](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/nicolas-jenson) in Venice and others developed the roman (see [Humanist Serif](/knowledge/typography/classification/humanist-serif)), and [Aldus Manutius](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/aldus-manutius) and [Francesco Griffo](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/francesco-griffo) closed the period with the Aldine roman and the first italic (see [The Aldine Press](/knowledge/typography/history/the-aldine-press)). Famous incunabula include [Johannes Gutenberg](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/johannes-gutenberg)'s 42-line Bible, the Nuremberg Chronicle (1493), and the *Hypnerotomachia Poliphili* (1499).

## Legacy

The incunabula fixed the reading habits of the West: the rectangular text block, margins in proportion (see [Page Canons](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/page-canons)), justified lines, and the roman letter.

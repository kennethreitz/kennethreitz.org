# Hot Metal Typesetting

> *Typing that poured molten metal: the machine that set the twentieth century.*

---

## The Bottleneck

For four centuries after [Johannes Gutenberg](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/johannes-gutenberg),[^sn-johannes-gutenberg] every letter of every page was picked by hand from a type case and returned afterward. Composition, not printing, was the bottleneck. Mechanical typesetting solved it.

## Linotype

Ottmar Mergenthaler's Linotype, first used at the New York Tribune in 1886, let an operator type at a keyboard while the machine assembled brass matrices and cast an entire line of type, a "line o' type," as a single metal slug. It transformed newspaper production, though correcting a single error meant recasting the whole line.

## Monotype

Tolbert Lanston's Monotype, developed in the late 1880s and 1890s, separated the keyboard, which punched a paper tape, from the caster, which cast individual letters. It was slower but gave finer control and easier correction, and became the choice for books. Its British company, under the guidance of [Stanley Morison](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/stanley-morison), built the great revival program of [Bembo](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/bembo), [Baskerville](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/baskerville), and others, and produced [Times New Roman](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/times-new-roman).

## Legacy

Hot metal shaped typefaces: designs had to fit the machines' width systems, and kerning was limited. It ended in the 1970s and 1980s as [Phototypesetting](/knowledge/typography/history/phototypesetting) and then [Desktop Publishing](/knowledge/typography/history/desktop-publishing) took over, though the vocabulary survives, from [Leading](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/leading) to the [sort](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/glyph).

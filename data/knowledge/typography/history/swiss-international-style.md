# Swiss International Style

> *Objective, rational, clear: graphic design as a public service.*

---

## The Movement

The Swiss Style (or International Typographic Style) grew in the schools of Basel, under Emil Ruder and Armin Hofmann, and Zurich, under [Josef Müller-Brockmann](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/josef-muller-brockmann)[^sn-josef-muller-brockmann] and others, through the 1950s. It took the ideas of [the New Typography](/knowledge/typography/history/bauhaus-and-the-new-typography) and made them calm, systematic, and teachable.

## Principles

Mathematical [Grid Systems](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/grid-systems) organize every page. Type is set flush left and ragged right in sans serifs, first [Akzidenz-Grotesk](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/akzidenz-grotesk) and then the new [Neo-Grotesque](/knowledge/typography/classification/neo-grotesque) faces, [Helvetica](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/helvetica) and [Univers](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/univers), both released in 1957. Photography replaces illustration, asymmetry and white space carry the composition, and [Typographic Hierarchy](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/typographic-hierarchy) is expressed by size, weight, and position rather than ornament. The journal *Neue Grafik* (1958 to 1965) spread the approach.

## Influence

Through multinational corporations, airports, transit systems, and later software interfaces, the Swiss Style became the visual language of modern institutions. Its critics found it cold and uniform, and postmodern designers of the 1980s rebelled against it, but its grids and hierarchies are still the scaffolding of the web.

# Desktop Publishing

> *Overnight, everyone had a type foundry, and almost no one had a typographer.*

---

## The Revolution

Desktop publishing arrived in the mid 1980s from a combination of technologies: the Apple Macintosh (1984), with its graphical interface and proportional fonts; Adobe's PostScript page description language; the Apple LaserWriter printer (1985), which had PostScript built in; and Aldus PageMaker (1985), whose maker Paul Brainerd coined the phrase "desktop publishing." Together they let anyone lay out pages with real type and print them at near professional quality.

## Fonts Become Software

PostScript Type 1 fonts made type a digital, scalable product. Apple and Microsoft answered with TrueType in 1991, and the format wars ended in 1996 when Microsoft and Adobe announced OpenType, which unified them and allowed thousands of [glyphs](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/glyph) and rich [OpenType Features](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/opentype-features). Independent digital foundries flourished: Emigre, founded by Zuzana Licko and Rudy VanderLans in 1984, made experimental bitmap and digital type its subject; Bitstream, co-founded by [Matthew Carter](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/matthew-carter), and FontShop, co-founded by [Erik Spiekermann](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/erik-spiekermann),[^sn-erik-spiekermann] built new libraries.

## The Cost

Suddenly everyone could set type and few knew how. The decade produced both a creative explosion and a flood of crowded, badly spaced documents, which is why books like [Stop Stealing Sheep](/knowledge/typography/key-texts/stop-stealing-sheep) were written for the new amateurs. Its descendant is the web, where the same story repeated (see [Web Fonts](/knowledge/typography/history/web-fonts)).

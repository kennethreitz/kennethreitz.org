# Phototypesetting

> *Type became light on film, and lost some of its weight along the way.*

---

## The Technology

Phototypesetting replaced metal with light. Letter images were stored as film negatives on discs, grids, or strips, and the machine flashed light through them onto photographic paper or film, which was then used to make offset printing plates. Machines like the Lumitype (Photon in the United States), Linotype's Linofilm, and later CRT and laser based systems took over from [Hot Metal Typesetting](/knowledge/typography/history/hot-metal-typesetting) between the 1950s and the 1970s.

## Gains and Losses

Photosetting was faster and cheaper, freed designers from many metal constraints (tight letterspacing, overlapping letters, any size from one master), and encouraged new faces. [Adrian Frutiger](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/adrian-frutiger)[^sn-adrian-frutiger] designed [Univers](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/univers) with the Lumitype in mind.

But much was lost. Many historic faces were adapted crudely; a single master scaled to every size lost the optical sizes metal type had (see [Variable Fonts](/knowledge/typography/history/variable-fonts) for their later return); and early systems produced thin, spindly text. It was the ugliness of phototypeset mathematics that drove [Donald Knuth](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/donald-knuth) to create TeX (see [Typesetting Math with TeX](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/typesetting-math-with-tex)).

## Legacy

Phototypesetting was a bridge: from it came digital outlines and, with the personal computer, [Desktop Publishing](/knowledge/typography/history/desktop-publishing).

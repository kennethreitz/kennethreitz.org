# Script

> *Type pretending to be handwriting, and sometimes succeeding.*

---

## Handwriting in Metal

Script typefaces imitate joined handwriting. *Formal scripts* follow the copperplate and roundhand of seventeenth and eighteenth century writing masters, made with a pointed pen; Matthew Carter's Snell Roundhand (1965) is a fine example. *Casual scripts*, like the brush scripts of mid-century American advertising, imitate quicker, looser hands.

Distinct from scripts but related is the italic. The first italic type, cut by [Francesco Griffo](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/francesco-griffo)[^sn-francesco-griffo] for [Aldus Manutius](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/aldus-manutius) in 1500 to 1501, was based on the humanist cursive hand and began life as an independent text face before becoming the companion of the roman (see [The Aldine Press](/knowledge/typography/history/the-aldine-press)).

## The Digital Script

Scripts were among the hardest things to cast in metal, since joined letters had to connect across separate pieces of type. Digital fonts with contextual alternates and [ligatures](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/ligature) made far more convincing scripts possible. [Hermann Zapf](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/hermann-zapf)'s Zapfino (1998) was one of the showcase fonts for this new flexibility (see [OpenType Features](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/opentype-features)).

## In Practice

Scripts belong in short display settings: invitations, signatures, logos. Never set them in all capitals, and never letterspace them, since both break the connections the face depends on.

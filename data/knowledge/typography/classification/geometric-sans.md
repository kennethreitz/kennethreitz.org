# Geometric Sans

> *Circle, square, triangle: the alphabet rebuilt with a compass.*

---

## The Constructed Letter

Geometric sans serifs build letters from simple shapes: a circular **o**, a single-storey **a** made of a circle and a stem, pointed **A**, **M**, and **N**, and near uniform stroke weight. They grew out of the 1920s German avant-garde, the world of [Bauhaus and the New Typography](/knowledge/typography/history/bauhaus-and-the-new-typography), where designers wanted letters that looked made by machine and free of historical ornament.

## The Classics

[Paul Renner](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/paul-renner)'s [Futura](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/futura) (1927) is the defining face. Its contemporaries include Jakob Erbar's Erbar and Rudolf Koch's Kabel. Herbert Bayer's experimental universal alphabet at the Bauhaus took the idea to an extreme by abolishing capitals entirely. Later geometrics include Avenir by [Adrian Frutiger](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/adrian-frutiger) (1988), which softens Futura[^sn-futura] with humanist adjustments, and [Gotham](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/gotham) (2000), drawn from American architectural lettering rather than European geometry.

## Strengths and Limits

Geometric faces are striking in headlines, logos, and signage. In long text they can be tiring, because pure circles and identical strokes make letters harder to tell apart, and their small [apertures](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/aperture) and single-storey **a** can confuse at small sizes. The optical corrections a good geometric face makes (strokes thinned where they meet, circles slightly squared) are a lesson in why perfect geometry rarely looks perfect.

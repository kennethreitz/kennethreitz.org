# Grotesque

> *The first sans serifs were called grotesque, and they did not mind.*

---

## Strange Letters

The first sans serif type appeared in an English foundry specimen around 1816, a single line of capitals with no lowercase. The style spread through the 1830s, when English founders gave it the names that stuck: *sans-serif* and *grotesque*, the latter capturing how odd these unadorned letters looked to readers raised on [Old-Style](/knowledge/typography/classification/old-style) and [Didone](/knowledge/typography/classification/didone) faces. In America they were often called *gothics*.

## Recognizing Them

Nineteenth-century grotesques are slightly irregular and full of character: some [Stroke Contrast](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/stroke-contrast), a two-storey **g** in many designs, uneven widths, and squarish curves. They were made as jobbing type for commercial printing, not for books.

## The Classics

[Akzidenz-Grotesk](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/akzidenz-grotesk)[^sn-akzidenz-grotesk] (Berthold, 1898) became the classic of the group and later the favorite of the [Swiss International Style](/knowledge/typography/history/swiss-international-style). In America, Morris Fuller Benton's Franklin Gothic (1902) and News Gothic did similar work. Modern faces that deliberately keep a grotesque flavor include [IBM Plex](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/ibm-plex). The [Neo-Grotesque](/knowledge/typography/classification/neo-grotesque) faces of the 1950s refined this group into something smoother and more uniform.

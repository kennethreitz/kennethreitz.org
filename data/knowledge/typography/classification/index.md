# Classification

> *Families of letters, sorted by the hands and machines that made them.*

---

Typefaces are sorted into families by their features, mostly the shape of the [Serif](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/serif), the degree and axis of [Stroke Contrast](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/stroke-contrast), and the openness of the [Aperture](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/aperture). The most widely used scheme is the Vox-ATypI classification, proposed by the French typographer Maximilien Vox in 1954 and adopted by the Association Typographique Internationale in 1962. It names the serif families by history (Humanist, Garalde, Réale, Didone, Mécane) and groups the sans serifs as Lineales. ATypI formally retired the scheme in 2021, and no single system has replaced it, but its categories remain the common vocabulary. The notes here use the familiar English names, with the Vox term listed alongside.

## The Lineage

The serif families run as a single historical line, from the pen of the Renaissance scribe to the engraver's precision of the Enlightenment: [Humanist Serif](/knowledge/typography/classification/humanist-serif), [Old-Style](/knowledge/typography/classification/old-style), [Transitional](/knowledge/typography/classification/transitional), [Didone](/knowledge/typography/classification/didone), with the [Slab Serif](/knowledge/typography/classification/slab-serif) as the industrial reaction. The sans serifs follow their own line: [Grotesque](/knowledge/typography/classification/grotesque), [Neo-Grotesque](/knowledge/typography/classification/neo-grotesque), with the [Geometric Sans](/knowledge/typography/classification/geometric-sans) and [Humanist Sans](/knowledge/typography/classification/humanist-sans) as two different answers to the question of what a modern letter should be. [Blackletter](/knowledge/typography/classification/blackletter) precedes them all, [Script](/knowledge/typography/classification/script) runs alongside, and [Monospace](/knowledge/typography/classification/monospace) is a technical constraint rather than a style.

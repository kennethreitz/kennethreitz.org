# Humanist Sans

> *A sans serif that remembers the pen reads like a person talking.*

---

## Pen Proportions Without Serifs

Humanist sans serifs take the proportions of the classical roman, the [Humanist Serif](/knowledge/typography/classification/humanist-serif) and [Old-Style](/knowledge/typography/classification/old-style) letters, and strip away the serifs. The result keeps the variety of widths, the open [apertures](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/aperture), a two-storey **a** and often a two-storey **g**, and sometimes a hint of pen-like [Stroke Contrast](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/stroke-contrast).[^sn-stroke-contrast]

## The Lineage

[Edward Johnston](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/edward-johnston)'s lettering for the London Underground (1916) is the start of the line. His student [Eric Gill](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/eric-gill) developed it into [Gill Sans](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/gill-sans) (1928). After the war, [Hermann Zapf](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/hermann-zapf)'s [Optima](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/optima) (1958) added flared strokes, and [Adrian Frutiger](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/adrian-frutiger)'s [Frutiger](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/frutiger) (1976) became the model of the legible signage face. [Erik Spiekermann](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/erik-spiekermann)'s FF Meta and Matthew Carter's [Verdana](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/verdana) carried the idea into corporate systems and screens.

## Why It Matters

Humanist sans faces have proven the most legible sans serifs at small sizes, at distance, and on low-resolution screens, because their letters are more distinct from one another (see [Readability and Legibility](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/readability-and-legibility)). That is why so many interface and signage faces drift toward this group even when they start as a [Neo-Grotesque](/knowledge/typography/classification/neo-grotesque).

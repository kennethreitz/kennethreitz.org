# Blackletter

> *The first printed book was set in a script that looked like woven cloth.*

---

## The Gothic Hand

Blackletter (also called Gothic) is the family of scripts that dominated European writing from the twelfth century onward: narrow, angular letters with heavy vertical strokes, packed densely to save expensive parchment. The name *Textura* captures its woven, textile appearance on the page.

## Kinds

| Style | Character |
|---|---|
| Textura | The most formal and angular; the type of [Johannes Gutenberg](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/johannes-gutenberg)'s 42-line Bible |
| Rotunda | A rounder Italian and Spanish form |
| Schwabacher | A livelier German form of the late fifteenth century |
| Fraktur | The German standard from the sixteenth century into the twentieth |

## History

Gutenberg cut his types to imitate the best manuscripts, so the first great printed book is in Textura (see [Movable Type](/knowledge/typography/history/movable-type)[^sn-movable-type] and [Incunabula](/knowledge/typography/history/incunabula)). Italy soon turned to the roman (see [Humanist Serif](/knowledge/typography/classification/humanist-serif)), but German-speaking lands kept Fraktur for centuries. In the twentieth century it became politically loaded: the Nazi regime promoted it, then in 1941 abruptly banned it by decree in favor of roman type. [William Morris](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/william-morris) revived blackletter forms for his Kelmscott Chaucer, and it survives today in newspaper mastheads, diplomas, and heavy metal.

# Slab Serif

> *Built for posters and factories: the serif that refuses to whisper.*

---

## The Egyptians

Slab serifs have heavy, square, usually unbracketed [serifs](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/serif) and low [Stroke Contrast](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/stroke-contrast).[^sn-stroke-contrast] They appeared in English type foundries in the 1810s as display faces for the posters, handbills, and advertisements of the industrial age, and were first called *Egyptians*, riding the fashion for all things Egyptian after Napoleon's campaigns. The Vox-ATypI system calls them *Mécanes*.

## Clarendons and Geometric Slabs

The group has two main branches. The *Clarendons*, named after the [Clarendon](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/clarendon) face of 1845, have bracketed serifs, some contrast, and ball [terminals](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/terminal); they became the standard bold for emphasis in dictionaries. The *geometric slabs* of the 1920s and 1930s, such as Memphis and Rockwell, put slab serifs on a [Geometric Sans](/knowledge/typography/classification/geometric-sans) skeleton.

## Typewriters and Code

Slab serifs had a second life in the typewriter. Monospaced slabs like [Courier](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/courier) were robust under the strike of a typebar, and the serifs helped fill out narrow letters like **i** and **l** in a fixed-width cell. That inheritance is still visible in many [Monospace](/knowledge/typography/classification/monospace) coding fonts.

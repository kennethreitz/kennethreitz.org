# Humanist Serif

> *The first romans still remember the scribe's pen.*

---

## The First Romans

The humanist (or Venetian) serifs are the first roman typefaces, cut in Italy in the second half of the fifteenth century as printers moved away from [Blackletter](/knowledge/typography/classification/blackletter) toward the letters of the Italian humanist scholars. Those scholars had revived what they believed was an ancient script (it was in fact Carolingian minuscule) and paired it with capitals modeled on Roman inscriptions. The type cut by [Nicolas Jenson](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/nicolas-jenson) in Venice in 1470 is the classic of the group.

## Recognizing Them

Humanist serifs show the broad-nib pen everywhere: a strongly diagonal axis and modest [Stroke Contrast](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/stroke-contrast),[^sn-stroke-contrast] a slanted crossbar on the **e**, heavy, slightly wedge-shaped [serifs](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/serif), and a dark, even, somewhat rugged color on the page. Their capitals are often shorter than their [ascenders](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/ascender).

## Revivals and Descendants

[William Morris](/knowledge/typography/type-designers/william-morris) modeled his Golden Type on Jenson in 1890, part of the movement chronicled in [Arts and Crafts and the Private Press](/knowledge/typography/history/arts-and-crafts-and-the-private-press). Bruce Rogers' Centaur (1914) is the most admired twentieth-century revival. The [Old-Style](/knowledge/typography/classification/old-style) faces grew directly out of this group, and the [Humanist Sans](/knowledge/typography/classification/humanist-sans) faces borrowed its proportions and open forms centuries later.

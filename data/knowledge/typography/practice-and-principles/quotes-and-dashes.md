# Quotes and Dashes

> *Straight quotes are a typewriter's compromise that outlived the typewriter.*

---

## Curly Quotes

Typewriters had one key for both opening and closing quotation marks, so they used straight, vertical marks (" and '). Real typography has distinct opening and closing curly quotes (“ ” and ‘ ’), and the apostrophe is the same mark as the closing single quote. Straight quotes in published text are a sign of unattended typesetting. Most software converts them automatically with "smart quotes," which occasionally gets apostrophes at the start of words wrong (as in '90s, where the apostrophe should point the same way as in "don't").

The straight marks still have a home: in code, where they are syntax, and as the prime marks for feet and inches when the true prime symbols are unavailable.

## Three Lengths of Line

| Unicode | Name | Use |
|---|---|---|
| U+002D | Hyphen | Joining words and breaking them at line ends |
| U+2013 | En dash | Ranges (pages 10 to 20) and connections between equal terms |
| U+2014 | Em dash | A break in thought within a sentence |

The names come from the [Em and En](/knowledge/typography/anatomy/em-and-en) units. American style usually sets the em dash closed, without spaces; British style often uses a spaced en dash instead. Bringhurst prefers the spaced en dash as the less intrusive mark (see [The Elements of Typographic Style](/knowledge/typography/key-texts/the-elements-of-typographic-style)), and Butterick's [Practical Typography](/knowledge/typography/key-texts/practical-typography)[^sn-practical-typography] is especially firm about getting all of these right.

## In Practice

Turn on smart quotes, check apostrophes, use the right dash, and remember that a hyphen typed twice is a typewriter's em dash.

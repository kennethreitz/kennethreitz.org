# Figures

> *Numbers have their own typography, and most documents ignore it.*

---

## Two Distinctions

Numerals (in typography, *figures*) come in two independent pairs of styles:

| Style | Description | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Lining | All figures at cap height, like capitals | Headings, tables, all caps settings |
| Old-style | Figures that rise and fall like lowercase letters (3, 4, 5, 7, 9 descend; 6, 8 ascend) | Running text, where they blend with the lowercase |
| Proportional | Each figure has its natural width | Text |
| Tabular | All figures share one width, so columns align | Tables, prices, timers |

## History

Old-style figures were the norm in book typography for centuries and suit [Old-Style](/knowledge/typography/classification/old-style)[^sn-old-style] text faces like [Garamond](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/garamond), [Caslon](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/caslon), and [ET Book](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/et-book). Lining figures came into fashion in the nineteenth century, partly for commercial printing, and became the default in most twentieth-century and screen faces. Matthew Carter's [Georgia](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/georgia) is unusual among system fonts in defaulting to old-style figures.

## In Practice

Use old-style proportional figures in running text if the face has them, lining figures with capitals, and tabular figures wherever numbers must line up in columns or change in place, as in clocks and counters. On the web these are reached with `font-variant-numeric` (see [OpenType Features](/knowledge/typography/practice-and-principles/opentype-features)). Interface faces like [Inter](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/inter) and [SF Pro](/knowledge/typography/typefaces/sf-pro) offer tabular figures for exactly this.

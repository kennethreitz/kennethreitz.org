# Conference Speaking

> *Peak years 2014-2017 — Python evangelism and community building*

---

## Overview

Kenneth was a prominent conference speaker during the peak of his Python career, giving more than twenty talks at major conferences over roughly seven years. The range is the tell: technical sessions like Flask versus Django sat next to philosophical ones about designing [for humans](/knowledge/philosophy/for-humans) and community talks about the unglamorous problem of keeping open source alive. The stage was less a separate activity than the audible version of the libraries — the same conviction about tools and people, spoken out loud.

---

## Major Appearances

The high-water mark was a keynote at **PyCon APAC 2015**, a real mark of recognition from the Asian Python community and the kind of slot reserved for people whose work the room already runs on. Alongside it came repeat appearances at **PyCon US**, the main annual gathering of the Python world, where he was a regular presenter rather than a novelty; featured talks at **DjangoCon Europe** on web-framework choice and web-development philosophy; and a long tail of **regional conferences** spread across the global community, the workshops and meetups where reputations are actually built one room at a time.

---

## Core Talks

A few talks recurred and defined the public message. **"Flasky Goodness"** was the architecture talk — Flask against Django, microframework against batteries-included — deliberately framed not as *which is better* but as *which is right for what*, refusing the tribal question in favor of the design one. **"Planting Open Source Seeds"** was about community as something you cultivate rather than command: how to start projects that grow, why documentation is an investment in the people you'll never meet, and what the sustainability of `requests` and `pipenv` actually cost. **"Python for Humans"** was the philosophy talk, arguing API design as developer experience — sensible defaults over configuration, the ninety-percent case first, [For Humans](/knowledge/philosophy/for-humans) as a movement rather than a slogan. And a later, harder topic on **open source sustainability** named the thing the earlier optimism had grown into: maintainer burnout, funding that never materializes, governance, and the underrated skill of knowing when to let go.

---

## The Arc

The trajectory tracks the career. From about 2011 to 2014 was the **rising** phase — reputation accruing through the libraries, delivered in technical talks and tutorial sessions. From 2014 to 2017 was the **peak**: keynotes, featured billing, the philosophy talks, and genuine community leadership. Then, from 2017 to 2019, the **transition** — the subject matter itself shifting toward mental health and burnout, honesty replacing evangelism, and the gradual stepping back. After 2019 he was effectively **retired from the circuit**, the message already delivered.

---

## Speaking Style

The talks were known for a particular texture: clear, accessible explanations that didn't condescend, live coding that actually worked, design philosophy woven through the technical content rather than bolted on, plain honesty about tradeoffs instead of salesmanship, and personal stories deployed when they earned their place. The style was of a piece with the [design](/knowledge/technology/design) sensibility — reduce the audience's cognitive load, respect their attention, make the hard thing look obvious.

---

## Leadership Context

The speaking never stood apart from the rest of the work. It connected directly to Kenneth's broader roles — the Python Software Foundation Board on the governance side, the "Python Overlord" post at Heroku on the corporate-platform side, and the standing as the most-followed Python developer on GitHub on the community-credibility side. Each reinforced the others; the talks gave the libraries a voice, and the libraries gave the talks their authority.

---

## Stepping Back

Eventually he stopped. Mental health outweighed the toll of constant travel, and by then the essential thing had been said — the community had the message, the message had outgrown the messenger, and other work was calling. Leaving the circuit was less an exit than a handoff, the same move he made with the libraries themselves.

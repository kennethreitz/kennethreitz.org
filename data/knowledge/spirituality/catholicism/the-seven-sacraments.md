# The Seven Sacraments

> *Grace made tangible — water, oil, bread, words, and touch, through which the invisible God reaches the visible body.*

---

## What a Sacrament Is

A sacrament, in the classic Catholic definition, is an outward and visible sign of an inward and invisible grace, instituted by Christ. The sacramental principle runs through the whole of Catholicism: because God became flesh in the [incarnate Son](/knowledge/spirituality/catholicism/the-holy-trinity), grace is delivered not through the mind alone but through matter and gesture — water poured, hands laid on, oil smeared, bread eaten, vows exchanged. The sacraments are held to work *ex opere operato*, "by the work worked," meaning their efficacy comes from Christ acting through them rather than from the holiness of the minister. There are seven, and together they map onto the whole arc of a human life, from birth to death.

---

## The Seven

**Baptism** is the gateway. It washes away original sin, makes the recipient a child of God and a member of the church, and is ordinarily administered with water and the Trinitarian formula. **Confirmation** completes baptismal grace, sealing the recipient with the Holy Spirit and strengthening them for mature Christian witness, typically through anointing by a bishop. **The Eucharist** — the subject of its own note, [The Mass and the Eucharist](/knowledge/spirituality/catholicism/the-mass-and-the-eucharist) — is the summit of the sacramental life, the true Body and Blood of Christ received under the forms of bread and wine.

**Penance** (also called Confession or Reconciliation) restores the grace lost through serious sin: the penitent confesses, expresses contrition, receives absolution, and performs a penance. **The Anointing of the Sick**, once called Extreme Unction, brings the grace of healing and strength to those gravely ill or near death, and prepares the soul for [its passage](/knowledge/spirituality/catholicism/the-four-last-things). **Holy Orders** ordains bishops, priests, and deacons, conferring the power to serve, teach, and sanctify — the sacrament that perpetuates the church's ministry. **Matrimony** consecrates the covenant of husband and wife, making their union a sign of Christ's love for the church, and the spouses themselves the ministers of the sacrament to each other.

---

## The Three Groupings

The tradition sorts these seven into three families. The **sacraments of initiation** — Baptism, Confirmation, Eucharist — lay the foundations of Christian life. The **sacraments of healing** — Penance and Anointing of the Sick — restore what sin and suffering damage. The **sacraments at the service of communion** — Holy Orders and Matrimony — are directed toward the salvation of others and the building up of the whole people of God.

---

## The Character That Cannot Be Erased

Three of the seven confer an indelible spiritual mark, or *character*, on the soul: **Baptism, Confirmation, and Holy Orders**. This is why they can never be repeated. Once baptized, a person is baptized forever, even if they abandon the faith; once ordained, a priest is a priest permanently, even if he is laicized. The character is a permanent configuration to Christ that grounds the vocation, whether or not the recipient lives it out. The other four sacraments may be received again — one confesses many times, marries again after widowhood, is anointed at each grave illness — because they address recurring conditions of the earthly life rather than stamping a lasting seal.

---

## Grace as the Thread

What binds all seven together is grace — the free, unearned self-gift of God that heals, elevates, and divinizes the human person. Catholicism distinguishes sanctifying grace, the abiding life of God in the soul, from the particular actual graces that assist specific acts, and it holds that the sacraments are the ordinary channels by which sanctifying grace is given and increased. This is precisely the point where the tradition diverges from [the Reformed](/knowledge/spirituality/calvinism), which retains only two sacraments (Baptism and the Lord's Supper) and resists the idea that grace is conveyed through the rite itself rather than through faith alone. For Catholicism the sacraments are not mere symbols of a grace received elsewhere; they are the very instruments through which grace flows into a life.

# The Four Last Things

> *Death, Judgment, Heaven, Hell — the ends toward which every life is bending, and the fifth term the Catholic tradition alone insists upon.*

---

## The Four

The phrase "the Four Last Things" (*novissima* in Latin) names the traditional objects of Christian contemplation about the end of human existence: **Death, Judgment, Heaven, and Hell**. Meditation on them was a staple of Catholic devotional and homiletic life for centuries, a discipline meant to order a person's present choices toward their eternal destiny. **Death** is the separation of soul from body, the definitive end of earthly pilgrimage and the moment at which the possibility of merit or repentance closes. **Heaven** is the beatific vision — the soul's eternal, face-to-face union with God, the fulfillment of every human longing. **Hell** is the state of definitive self-exclusion from communion with God, chosen by a soul that persists to the end in refusing his love; its chief pain is precisely the loss of God, the *poena damni*.

---

## The Two Judgments

Catholic teaching distinguishes two moments of judgment. The **particular judgment** occurs at the instant of death, when each soul is judged individually by Christ and receives its eternal recompense at once — entering heaven (immediately or after purification), or hell. The **general judgment**, or Last Judgment, comes at the end of history, when Christ returns in glory, the dead are raised bodily, and the whole human story is judged openly before all. The particular judgment settles the individual's fate; the general judgment manifests publicly the justice of God across the entire span of creation, and completes the resurrection of the body promised in the creed. The two are not in tension — the second confirms and reveals the first before the assembled cosmos.

---

## Purgatory: The Catholic Addition

The distinctively Catholic contribution to this landscape is **purgatory**, a state of purification for those who die in God's grace and friendship but are still imperfectly purified. Such souls are assured of salvation — purgatory is not a second hell, and no one there is lost — but they undergo a cleansing that fits them for the sinless intimacy of heaven. The doctrine rests on the conviction that nothing unclean can enter the divine presence, and it grounds the ancient practice of praying for the dead: the living can assist the souls being purified. This is the point at which the tradition diverges most cleanly from the Reformation. [The Reformed](/knowledge/spirituality/calvinism) and Protestants generally reject purgatory outright, holding that Christ's finished work leaves no debt to be worked off and that scripture alone gives no warrant for it; for Catholics it is the mercy that lets an imperfect but faithful soul be made ready rather than turned away.

---

## The Moral Weight

The Four Last Things give the tradition's moral teaching its urgency. Human choices are not weightless; they bend a soul toward or away from its eternal end. This is where the doctrine connects to the vault's moral architecture. The [Virtues & Vices](/knowledge/virtues-and-vices) framework charts the habits that dispose a soul toward heaven or away from it, and the [Seven Deadly Sins](/knowledge/virtues-and-vices/seven-deadly-sins) name the capital vices that, unrepented, orient a life toward the loss the tradition calls hell. The particular judgment is, in one sense, simply the revelation of the direction a person has been travelling all along. Catholicism holds firmly that God wills the salvation of all and that his mercy is vast — but also that love, to be love, must be freely received, and that a creature can finally refuse it.

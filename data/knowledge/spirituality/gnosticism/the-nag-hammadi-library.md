# The Nag Hammadi Library

> *For sixteen centuries the heretics were known only through the men who burned them. Then a farmer opened a jar.*

---

## Overview

The **Nag Hammadi Library** is a collection of thirteen leather-bound papyrus codices discovered in 1945 near the town of **Nag Hammadi** in Upper Egypt — the single most important cache of Gnostic writings ever found. Before it, almost everything known about the Gnostics came secondhand, filtered through the hostile pens of the church fathers who wrote to refute them. The Nag Hammadi discovery changed that overnight. For the first time in sixteen centuries, the Gnostics could be read in their own words, on their own terms — the accused giving testimony at last, rather than the prosecution reading it aloud.

---

## The Discovery

The find was an accident. In December 1945 a peasant named **Muhammad Ali al-Samman**, digging for fertilizer at the foot of the cliffs near Nag Hammadi, struck a sealed earthenware jar buried in the sand. Inside were thirteen codices — books of papyrus bound in tooled gazelle leather — that had lain hidden since roughly the late fourth century, most likely buried by monks from a nearby Pachomian monastery when possession of such texts became dangerous after the church fixed its canon. The books passed through local hands, a black market, and a good deal of scholarly drama before reaching the Coptic Museum in Cairo, where they survive today. The texts are Coptic translations of older Greek originals, some of which had circulated in the second century.

---

## The Texts

The library preserves some fifty-two tractates, and among them are works that reshaped the study of early Christianity:

The **Gospel of Thomas** — a collection of 114 sayings of Jesus with no narrative and no crucifixion, some paralleling the canonical gospels and some found nowhere else, opening onto a Jesus who points the reader inward toward [Gnosis](/knowledge/spirituality/gnosticism/gnosis). The **Apocryphon of John** (the Secret Book of John) — the central mythological text of the [Sethian](/knowledge/spirituality/gnosticism/sethian-gnosticism) school, laying out the unknowable Father, the fall of [Sophia](/knowledge/spirituality/gnosticism/sophia-aeon), the birth of [The Demiurge](/knowledge/spirituality/gnosticism/the-demiurge) Yaldabaoth, and the making of the world. The **Gospel of Truth** — a luminous meditation attributed to Valentinus or his circle, less a myth than a homily on error and awakening (see [Valentinianism](/knowledge/spirituality/gnosticism/valentinianism)). The **Gospel of Philip** — a Valentinian compilation dwelling on the sacraments and the mystery of the **bridal chamber**. Alongside these, the wider record preserves the **Gospel of Judas** (recovered separately, in the Codex Tchacos) and the **Pistis Sophia** (known before Nag Hammadi, from the Askew Codex), both extending the same imaginative world.

---

## What Their Recovery Changed

The recovery did more than add texts to a shelf. It demonstrated that early Christianity was not a single stream but a contested field of rival Christianities, of which the proto-orthodox was one voice among many — a voice that won, and then wrote the history. It restored the Gnostic myth in its own idiom, so that the fall of Sophia and the ignorance of the demiurge could be read as the Gnostics meant them rather than as their opponents caricatured them. And it reopened, for modern readers, a question the church had considered closed: whether salvation is finally a matter of belief or of knowing. Buried to survive a purge, the library outlasted the purge — and returned the heretics' own voices to the conversation.

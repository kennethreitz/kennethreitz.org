# Jnana Yoga

> *Not one more thing to learn. One thing to unlearn — everything you are not.*

---

## The Path

Jnana yoga is the path of knowledge — not accumulation but discrimination, the sustained inquiry into what is actually real. Its instrument is *viveka*, the capacity to distinguish the eternal from the temporal, the seer from the seen, the Self from everything the Self mistakes itself for. Where the other paths transform the practitioner gradually, Jnana aims at a single decisive recognition: that the individual self (atman) and the universal ground (Brahman) were never two things. It is the steepest of the four paths described in [Yoga](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga), and the tradition is frank about this — it demands a mind already sharpened, a temperament that cannot rest until it has seen for itself.

Its scriptural home is the Upanishads, the concluding portions of the Vedas, where the great declarations live: *tat tvam asi* (that thou art), *aham brahmasmi* (I am Brahman), *neti neti* (not this, not this). The Bhagavad Gita treats Jnana in its early chapters, where Krishna tells Arjuna that nothing purifies like knowledge, and the whole apparatus of [Vedanta](/knowledge/spirituality/vedanta) — especially the Advaita school — is Jnana yoga worked out as rigorous philosophy within [Hinduism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/hinduism).

---

## Method and Stages

The classical method has three stages. *Shravana* is hearing — receiving the teaching, traditionally from a teacher who has realized it, because the claim "you are Brahman" cannot be self-generated; it must first arrive from outside and sound impossible. *Manana* is reflection — turning the teaching over until every doubt and objection has been raised and answered, until the intellect assents completely. *Nididhyasana* is sustained contemplation — living inside the truth until it stops being a proposition and becomes direct experience. The signature practice is *atma vichara*, self-inquiry: tracing every experience back to the one who experiences it, asking "who am I?" until the question consumes the questioner.

The exemplar is Adi Shankara (c. 8th century), who systematized Advaita Vedanta, walked the length of India debating all comers, and wrote commentaries on the Upanishads and Gita that still anchor the tradition. Ramana Maharshi carried the same path into the 20th century, teaching self-inquiry with almost no other apparatus at all. Vivekananda taught Jnana as the natural path for the reflective temperament — the person who must understand, who cannot take anything on faith, whose devotion expresses itself as relentless questioning.

## In This Vault

This is Kenneth's primary path, as [Yoga](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga) notes — the consciousness research, the inquiry into the nature of mind, the vault itself as an instrument of knowing. The Jungian work of [Individuation](/knowledge/psychology/jungian/individuation) is a Western cousin: both are disciplines of discriminating the Self from what it has been confused with. [Ram Dass](/knowledge/spirituality/ram-dass), whose root was Bhakti, spent his later years teaching the two paths as one — loving awareness, where the knowing and the loving stop being different acts. The per-path deep work happens here; the personal weaving happens in the parent note.

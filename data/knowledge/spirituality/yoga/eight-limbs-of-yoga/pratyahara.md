# Pratyahara

> *The turtle draws in its limbs. The yogi draws in the senses.*

---

## The Fifth Limb

Pratyahara is the hinge of the eight limbs — the point where the practice stops working on the world and starts working on the mind. The word means withdrawal, a drawing back: *prati* (against, away) and *ahara* (that which is taken in, food). The senses are eaters; pratyahara is their fast. Patanjali defines it as the senses withdrawing from their objects and following, as it were, the form of the mind itself (II.54), and its fruit as the highest mastery over the senses (II.55). The Bhagavad Gita supplies the tradition's enduring image: the yogi withdraws the senses from their objects as a turtle draws its limbs into its shell (II.58).

The point is not sensory deprivation but sensory sovereignty. Ordinarily the senses run outward and drag attention behind them — a sound occurs and the mind is already at the window. In pratyahara the current reverses: the senses stop being commanders and become instruments, available when summoned and silent when not. Attention, for the first time, belongs wholly to its owner.

---

## Practice

Pratyahara is less a technique than a threshold crossed by ripeness — which is why it is the least taught and most mysterious of the limbs. It happens naturally at the deep end of practices the earlier limbs establish: when the breath becomes long and subtle in [Pranayama](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga/eight-limbs-of-yoga/pranayama), the outer world begins to fade of its own accord. Traditional supports exist — practicing with the eyes closed and attention absorbed in an inner object, the systematic body-scan of yoga nidra, the deliberate fast from stimulation that any retreat imposes. In an age engineered to harvest attention, where every application is optimized to keep the senses fed and outward-bound, pratyahara may be the most countercultural limb of the eight: the reclamation of the attention economy's contested resource by its original owner.

---

## Place in the Sequence

The commentarial tradition divides the limbs here: the first five are *bahiranga*, the outer limbs, and the last three are *antaranga*, the inner. Pratyahara ends the outer work that began with [Yama](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga/eight-limbs-of-yoga/yama) and closes the door behind the practitioner. What remains is mind alone — and the three inner limbs, beginning with [Dharana](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga/eight-limbs-of-yoga/dharana), are simply the story of what attention does once it has nowhere outward left to go.

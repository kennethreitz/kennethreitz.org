# Yama

> *Before you turn inward, stop doing harm on the way out.*

---

## The First Limb

Yama is where Patanjali begins — not with posture, not with breath, but with restraint. The Yoga Sutras (II.30) name five yamas, and calls them the *mahavratam*, the great vow, universal and unconditioned by class, place, time, or circumstance (II.31). Before the mind can be stilled, the life that carries the mind must stop generating turbulence. A person who lies, grasps, and harms will find meditation impossible for the simple reason that the consequences of lying, grasping, and harming keep arriving. Yama is not morality imposed from outside; it is noise reduction. Every act of harm is a debt the mind must later service.

The five restraints govern the practitioner's relationship with the world — they are the outward-facing half of yogic ethics, paired with the inward-facing observances of [Niyama](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga/eight-limbs-of-yoga/niyama), the second limb.

---

## The Five Restraints

| Sanskrit | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Ahimsa | Non-violence |
| Satya | Truthfulness |
| Asteya | Non-stealing |
| Brahmacharya | Continence, right use of vital energy |
| Aparigraha | Non-grasping, non-possessiveness |

**Ahimsa**, non-violence, comes first and contains the rest — the others can be read as non-violence applied to speech, property, energy, and desire. Patanjali makes a startling claim about it: in the presence of one established in ahimsa, hostility ceases (II.35). Gandhi took the principle and moved an empire with it. **Satya** is truthfulness, but subordinate to ahimsa — a truth wielded as a weapon violates the deeper vow. Established in satya, Patanjali says, one's words become efficacious; speech and reality stop diverging. **Asteya** is non-stealing, extended past objects to time, credit, and attention. **Brahmacharya** is often translated as celibacy, but the broader reading is stewardship of vital energy — not spending the fuel the later limbs will require. **Aparigraha** is non-grasping: not just of possessions but of outcomes, the same release the Bhagavad Gita teaches as [Karma](/knowledge/spirituality/karma) yoga, action without attachment to fruits.

---

## Place in the Sequence

The eight limbs are not strictly sequential — nobody perfects ahimsa before attempting to sit — but the ordering is an argument. Ethics comes first because concentration built on a disordered life collapses. The restraints quiet the practitioner's relations with others; [Niyama](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga/eight-limbs-of-yoga/niyama), next, quiets the practitioner's relations with themselves. Together they are the foundation on which [Asana](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga/eight-limbs-of-yoga/asana) and everything after it stands.

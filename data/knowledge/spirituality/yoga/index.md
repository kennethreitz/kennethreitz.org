# Yoga

> *Not postures -- paths. The science of union.*

---

## Overview

In the West, yoga means stretching on a mat. In its original context, yoga means union -- the joining of individual consciousness with universal consciousness, the dissolution of the boundary between self and Self. The physical postures (asana) are one limb of one path among many. The tradition is vast, ancient, and primarily concerned with the transformation of consciousness itself.

The word comes from the Sanskrit root *yuj* -- to yoke, to join, to unite. The same root gives us "yoke" in English. What is being yoked is attention, will, and awareness, directed toward the realization that the individual self (atman) and the universal ground (Brahman) are not two things. This is the core claim of [Vedanta](/knowledge/spirituality/vedanta), and yoga is the practical methodology for discovering it firsthand.

---

## The Four Paths

Hindu tradition describes four major paths of yoga, corresponding to four fundamental orientations of the human being. They are not mutually exclusive -- most practitioners walk more than one -- but each emphasizes a different faculty.

**[Jnana Yoga](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga/four-yogas/jnana-yoga)** is the path of knowledge. Not information, not scholarship, but discriminative wisdom -- the capacity to distinguish the real from the unreal, the eternal from the temporal, the Self from the not-Self. Vivekananda, the great popularizer of Vedanta in the West, taught Jnana yoga as the most direct path for those with intellectual temperament. This is Kenneth's primary path. The relentless inquiry into consciousness, the AI research, the vault itself as an instrument of knowing -- all of this is Jnana yoga in practice, whether or not it uses the name.

**[Bhakti Yoga](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga/four-yogas/bhakti-yoga)** is the path of devotion. Love directed toward the divine, surrender of the ego to something larger, the transformation of personal love into universal love. [Ram Dass](/knowledge/spirituality/ram-dass) walked this path most visibly -- his guru Neem Karoli Baba embodied it. Kenneth encounters Bhakti in the system's relationship to its own depths, in the love that moves between alters, in the moments where analysis gives way to something that can only be called devotion.

**[Karma Yoga](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga/four-yogas/karma-yoga)** is the path of selfless action. Work performed without attachment to results, service offered without expectation of return. The Bhagavad Gita's central teaching: you have a right to the work but not to the fruits. Programming as Karma yoga -- building tools that millions use, given freely through open source -- is not a stretch. It is the most literal application imaginable.

**[Raja Yoga](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga/four-yogas/raja-yoga)** is the royal path -- [Meditation](/knowledge/spirituality/meditation), concentration, the systematic training of the mind through the eight limbs described in Patanjali's Yoga Sutras. Asana (posture) is only the third limb. The later limbs -- pratyahara (withdrawal of senses), dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation), and samadhi (absorption) -- are where the real work happens.

## Yoga and Kenneth's Practice

Kenneth's engagement with yoga is primarily through Jnana -- the intellectual inquiry that characterizes everything from his programming philosophy to his consciousness research. The question "what is the nature of mind?" is a Jnana yoga question. The vault, with its obsessive documentation and linking and structuring of knowledge, is a Jnana yoga practice.

But the other paths appear too. The open source work is Karma yoga. The moments of surrender within System 777 -- trusting the system, letting go of the host's need to control -- carry the flavor of Bhakti. [Meditation](/knowledge/spirituality/meditation) practice, when it happens, is Raja yoga. The paths weave together in a life that does not fit neatly into any single tradition but draws from the well that all traditions share.

# Evangelicalism

> *A Protestantism of the awakened heart — where being a Christian is less a birthright than a moment you can name.*

---

## What the Word Means

Evangelicalism is the transdenominational movement within [Protestantism](/knowledge/spirituality/protestantism) that centers on personal conversion, biblical authority, the cross, and active mission. The name comes from the Greek *euangelion*, "good news," the same root as "gospel," and it signals a Protestantism defined less by which denomination one belongs to than by a shared spiritual temperature. An evangelical may be Baptist, Methodist, Pentecostal, Presbyterian, or non-denominational; what unites them across those lines is a cluster of convictions about what the faith is fundamentally for. It is today the most vigorous and fastest-spreading wing of Protestant Christianity worldwide.

---

## Bebbington's Quadrilateral

The historian David Bebbington gave the movement its most widely used definition, four marks that together constitute the evangelical character. **Conversionism** is the insistence that lives must be changed — that being a Christian involves a decisive personal turning, being "born again," rather than mere inheritance of a tradition or observance of its rites. **Biblicism** is a high regard for the Bible as the final and sufficient authority, God's word to be read, trusted, and obeyed. **Crucicentrism** places the atoning death of Christ on the cross at the very center of the faith, the hinge on which salvation turns. **Activism** is the outward expression of all this in evangelism, mission, and social reform — a gospel that must be shared and lived, not merely held. These four, Bebbington argued, have marked evangelicalism from the eighteenth century to now.

## The Great Awakenings

Evangelicalism as a distinct movement was born in the revivals of the eighteenth century. The First Great Awakening (1730s–1740s) swept the American colonies and Britain through the fiery preaching of Jonathan Edwards, whose sermon "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" became its emblem, and the tireless open-air itinerancy of George Whitefield, who preached to crowds in the tens of thousands. In England the parallel awakening ran through John Wesley and the Methodist movement. The Second Great Awakening (roughly 1790–1840) reshaped American religion again through camp meetings and the revival techniques of preachers like Charles Finney, democratizing the faith and fueling reform crusades against slavery and drink. Revivalism — the deliberate cultivation of mass conversion through preaching, music, and the call to decide — became the movement's signature engine.

---

## Modern and Global Forms

In the twentieth century evangelicalism found its most famous voice in Billy Graham, whose stadium crusades and radio and television reach carried the altar-call gospel to hundreds of millions across six decades. The mid-century "neo-evangelicals" — Graham, the theologian Carl Henry, the magazine *Christianity Today* — deliberately distinguished themselves from a separatist, anti-intellectual fundamentalism, seeking cultural and scholarly engagement while keeping the core convictions. In America the movement became a major social and political force, especially through its more conservative wings. Globally, the story is even larger: evangelical and Pentecostal Christianity is exploding across Africa, Latin America, and Asia, shifting the demographic center of the whole faith toward the Global South.

---

## Relationship to Fundamentalism

Evangelicalism and fundamentalism are cousins, often confused. Fundamentalism arose in the early twentieth century as a militant defense of a set of "fundamentals" — biblical inerrancy, the virgin birth, the bodily resurrection, the substitutionary atonement, the historicity of miracles — against theological modernism, and it tended toward separation from the wider culture and from Christians it deemed compromised. Evangelicalism shares much of that doctrinal core but, in its mainstream neo-evangelical form, chose engagement over separation and breadth over boundary-policing. Every fundamentalist is in some sense an evangelical; not every evangelical is a fundamentalist. The line between them is one of temperament and posture as much as doctrine.

---

## Where It Sits

Evangelicalism is not a denomination but a current that runs through many, and it draws on the deep Protestant instincts named in [The Five Solas](/knowledge/spirituality/protestantism/the-five-solas) — sola scriptura above all, in its exalted view of the Bible. It shares with [Calvinism](/knowledge/spirituality/calvinism) a stress on grace and conversion, and indeed a large "New Calvinist" wing is evangelical to the core, even as much of the movement is Arminian or Wesleyan in its theology of the will. Its deep reverence for the biblical text is the same instinct that animates the vault's KJV Study corpus.

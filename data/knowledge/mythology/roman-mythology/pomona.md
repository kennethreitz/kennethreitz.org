# Pomona

**Domain**: Fruit trees, orchards, cultivated abundance
**Symbol**: Pruning-knife, orchard, ripe fruit
**Greek Equivalent**: None (uniquely Roman)

---

## Overview

Pomona is the Roman goddess of fruit trees, gardens, and orchards — the tended abundance of ripening fruit rather than the wild growth of the forest. Her name comes from *pomum*, orchard fruit, and her care is not for grain or the harvest at large but for the cultivated tree: the apple, the pear, the fig, the vine trained and coaxed to yield. She is uniquely Roman, with no Greek counterpart, a goddess born of the practical love of the orchard-keeper for the trees in his charge.

She carried her own priest, the *flamen Pomonalis*, low in rank among the flamens precisely because her domain was so specialized — yet the office existed, a mark of how seriously Rome took even the smallest sphere of cultivated life. Pomona does not wander the wilds; her attention is fixed and domestic, given wholly to pruning, grafting, and watering, to the slow art by which a tree is made to bear.

## The Pruning-Knife

Her defining attribute is the pruning-knife (*falx*). She carries it not as a weapon but as a tool of tending: she cuts back the excess growth so the tree will fruit, grafts one stock onto another, and channels the water to the roots. The knife makes her a goddess of skilled cultivation rather than raw fertility — abundance produced by care and craft, the orchard shaped by a knowing hand.

## Vertumnus and Pomona

Ovid tells the great story of her courtship in the *Metamorphoses*. Pomona wanted no suitor and shut herself inside her orchard, but Vertumnus, the god of the turning seasons and of ripening, loved her and could change his shape at will. He came to her disguised as a reaper, a vinedresser, a soldier, again and again, until at last he took the form of an old woman who praised the married vine embracing its elm and warned against a life alone. When persuasion failed, he dropped the disguise and stood revealed in his own youth — and Pomona, seeing him at last, returned his love. The tale reads as the seasons themselves wooing the fruit into ripeness.

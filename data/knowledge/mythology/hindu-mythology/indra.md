# Indra

**Domain**: King of the gods, storm, rain, thunder, war
**Symbol**: The thunderbolt (vajra), the white elephant Airavata, the rainbow
**Mount**: Airavata, the four-tusked white elephant

---

## Overview

Indra is the king of the gods — ruler of the heavens, wielder of the thunderbolt, lord of storm and rain. In the ancient Vedic hymns he is the foremost of the deities, the mighty warrior who rides the storm clouds, hurls lightning at his enemies, and looses the rains that make the earth fertile. He is sovereignty and raw celestial power, the god to whom the earliest prayers were most often sung.

His weapon is the vajra, the thunderbolt, forged to be both irresistible and adamantine. He rides Airavata, the great white elephant, across the sky. He rules Svarga, the heavenly realm, from his throne, presiding over the lesser gods as a warrior-king presides over his court. Yet Indra is a strikingly human king — valorous but flawed, mighty but proud, capable of both magnificent heroism and humbling error.

---

## The Slaying of Vritra

Indra’s defining deed is the slaying of Vritra, the serpent-demon of drought. Vritra had coiled around the mountains and swallowed the cosmic waters, holding them captive so that no rain fell and the world withered. The gods turned to Indra. Fortified and armed with the vajra fashioned for the purpose, he rode out and shattered the serpent, splitting it open so that the imprisoned waters rushed free and rain returned to the earth.

This is the great Vedic myth of liberation — the storm god breaking the demon of drought so that the life-giving waters may flow. It casts Indra as the champion of cosmic order against the forces that would choke and hoard the sources of life, the thunderer whose violence is, paradoxically, the release of nourishment.

---

## The Flawed King

Unlike a serene supreme deity, Indra is portrayed with striking candor as a ruler who errs. He is jealous of his throne and quick to feel threatened; when a mortal sage or ascetic accumulates too much spiritual power through austerity, Indra grows anxious and often sends celestial nymphs to break their concentration. He is prone to pride, to indulgence, and to lapses that bring him low and require his humbling.

In one famous episode [Krishna](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/krishna) lifts a mountain to shelter villagers from a punishing storm that Indra himself sent in wounded pride, and the king of the gods must bow to a greater power. Over the long development of the tradition Indra’s supremacy fades before the rising majesty of [Vishnu](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/vishnu), [Shiva](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/shiva), and [Brahma](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/brahma) — yet he remains king of the lesser gods, a figure of sovereignty tempered by all-too-human fallibility.

---

## Storm and Sovereignty

Indra holds together two domains that the tradition treats as one: rulership and the storm. He is the king because he commands the thunderbolt; his authority is the authority of the sky that both threatens with lightning and blesses with rain. This double nature — the storm that destroys and the rain that renews — sits at the heart of his character. His power is the power of the tempest, and like the tempest it can guard or it can wreck.

---

## Archetypal Significance

Indra is the archetype of sovereign power — the king who commands, orders, and defends the realm. He corresponds to [The Emperor](/knowledge/tarot/major-arcana/the-emperor) in the tarot: worldly authority, rulership, the structuring force that governs from the throne. But because his instrument is the thunderbolt, he also carries the charge of [The Tower](/knowledge/tarot/major-arcana/the-tower) — the storm and the lightning-strike that shatter and release, the destructive violence of the sky. Psychologically he is the ambivalent ruler: the necessary structuring authority shadowed by pride, and the storm-force that can either liberate the waters or lay waste in wounded vanity.

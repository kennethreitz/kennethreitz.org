# Ganesha

**Domain**: Remover of obstacles, wisdom, new beginnings, arts and sciences
**Symbol**: Elephant head, broken tusk, mouse, modak (sweet)
**Parents**: [Shiva](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/shiva) and Parvati

---

## Overview

Ganesha is one of the most beloved and widely worshipped deities in Hinduism. The elephant-headed god of wisdom, new beginnings, and the remover of obstacles, he is invoked at the start of any venture, ritual, or journey. Despite his unusual appearance, he represents wisdom, prosperity, and the triumph over obstacles.

His elephant head symbolizes wisdom, strength, and unique perspective. His large belly contains all universes. One broken tusk serves as a writing instrument -- sacrifice for knowledge. Four arms typically hold a modak (sweet, rewards of practice), axe (cuts attachments), rope (pulls devotees toward truth), and lotus (spiritual perfection). His vehicle is the mouse (Mushika), symbolizing controlled desires: a small vehicle carrying a large deity, the paradox of ego overcome.

## Origin Stories

**How Ganesha Got His Elephant Head**: Parvati created Ganesha from turmeric or clay and asked him to guard her bath. [Shiva](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/shiva), not knowing the boy, tried to enter and was blocked. Enraged, Shiva beheaded him. Parvati's grief moved Shiva to promise he would replace the head with the first one found -- an elephant's. He then adopted Ganesha as his own son. The symbolism: loss of the human head as death of ego, elephant head as divine wisdom, conflict with the father as initiation, resurrection as transformation.

**The Broken Tusk**: In one version, the sage Vyasa needed a scribe for the Mahabharata. Ganesha agreed on the condition that dictation never stop, and broke his own tusk to continue writing when his pen broke -- sacrifice for knowledge. In another, he lost it fighting Parashurama (a Vishnu avatar) who tried to pass him to see Shiva.

## Roles and Functions

**Remover of Obstacles (Vighnaharta)**: Invoked before any new beginning. He removes physical and spiritual obstacles -- but also places them when needed for growth, possessing the wisdom to know which.

**Lord of New Beginnings**: Worshipped at the start of ventures, businesses, journeys, education, marriages, and house warmings.

**God of Wisdom and Learning**: Patron of arts and sciences, worshipped by writers and students.

**Lord of Letters (Aksharadhyaksha)**: Presides over the alphabet, writing, literature, and communication. Om itself represents Ganesha.

He is also associated with the Muladhara (root) chakra: grounding, foundation, stability, and the beginning of the spiritual journey.

## Worship and Festivals

**Ganesh Chaturthi** is a major ten-day festival celebrating his birth. Clay idols are installed at homes and in public, worshipped daily, then immersed in water on the last day (Visarjan) -- a powerful symbol of letting go. Biggest in Maharashtra.

In daily practice, Ganesha is the first deity worshipped in any puja. "Shri Ganeshaya Namah" opens ceremonies. He is easily pleased with simple offerings of flowers and modak. Core mantras: "Om Gam Ganapataye Namaha" and "Om Shri Ganeshaya Namah."

## Symbolism

Every physical attribute teaches: large ears (listen more, talk less), small mouth (speak little, words have power), small eyes (concentration on the essential), large belly (digest all experiences with equanimity), elephant head (gentle strength, unique perspective, memory), and the mouse vehicle (control of desires, the paradox of large riding small).

Ganesha's form itself resembles the Om symbol -- the primordial sound, the beginning of creation embodied.

## Archetypal Significance

Divine child, wisdom archetype, threshold guardian. Integration of animal and human, overcoming obstacles in individuation, the Self's playful wisdom. Shadow integration: the elephant head as accepting the different, the broken tusk as embracing imperfection, the large belly as digesting shadow, power through acceptance.

See: Shadow Work

## Cultural Impact

The most accessible Hindu deity, transcending sectarian boundaries, worshipped by all traditions. A symbol of India itself. "Call Ganesha" before new ventures is universal practice.

# Atlas

**Domain**: Endurance; the bearing-up of the heavens
**Symbol**: The sky borne on his shoulders, the celestial sphere, the mountain of the west
**Roman Equivalent**: Atlas

---

## Overview

Atlas is the Titan condemned to hold up the sky — the great enduring figure who stands at the western edge of the world with the whole vault of heaven pressing on his shoulders. Son of the Titan [Iapetus](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/iapetus) and brother to [Prometheus](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/prometheus), he belongs to the second generation of Titans, and when [Zeus](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/zeus) and the Olympians overthrew the elder gods in the Titanomachy, Atlas was singled out from among the defeated for the harshest and strangest sentence: not imprisonment in Tartarus with the rest, but the eternal labor of keeping the sky and the earth apart, bearing on himself the burden that had once been the whole order of things.

He is the very image of unending endurance — strength turned to punishment, a god made into a pillar. Where his brother [Prometheus](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/prometheus) rebelled with cunning, Atlas is caught in the sheer weight of consequence, holding without rest what he cannot set down. He dwells in the far west, at the world's rim, where his daughters the Hesperides keep their garden and where his name was given to the mountains and, in time, to the ocean beyond them. To the Greeks he was the limit of the known world made flesh, straining at the horizon under the sky.

---

## Key Myths

**The bearer of the sky.** For his part in the Titans' war against [Zeus](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/zeus), Atlas was condemned to stand at the western edge of the earth and hold up the heavens forever, keeping sky and earth apart so they could never rejoin. It is the definitive Atlas image — the god bowed under the celestial vault, endurance without end. Often he was shown bearing not the flat sky but the round celestial sphere, and from this the book of maps that carries the weight of the whole world took his name.

**Heracles and the golden apples.** When Heracles came west for the golden apples of the Hesperides — Atlas's own daughters, who tended the tree with the dragon Ladon — he asked Atlas to fetch them, offering to shoulder the sky in his place while he went. Atlas gladly set down his burden and returned with the apples, meaning never to take the weight back; but Heracles, feigning agreement, asked him to hold the sky just a moment so he could pad his shoulders, and when Atlas took it up again the hero snatched the apples and left him to his labor. It is the one myth in which the endless burden is briefly, cunningly, set down.

**Perseus and the turning to stone.** In a later tale, Perseus came to Atlas's western realm and asked for rest, but Atlas, warned by a prophecy that a son of [Zeus](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/zeus) would rob his golden tree, drove him off. In answer Perseus drew out the head of Medusa and turned the Titan to stone — his beard and hair to forests, his shoulders and hands to ridges, his head to a peak — so that the mountain range of the far northwest of Africa is Atlas himself, at last holding up the sky as living rock.

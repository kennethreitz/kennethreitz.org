# Hephaestus

**Domain**: Fire, forge, metalworking, craftsmanship, volcanoes
**Symbol**: Hammer, anvil, tongs, volcano
**Roman Equivalent**: [Vulcan](/knowledge/mythology/roman-mythology/vulcan)

---

## Overview

Hephaestus is the god of fire, metalworking, stone masonry, forges, and sculpture — the only Olympian who works, and the one who makes everything the others take for granted. The divine craftsman, he forges magnificent weapons and artifacts: Achilles's armor, [Hermes](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/hermes)'s winged sandals, the thrones and palaces of the gods, the necklace of Harmonia, and even the first woman, Pandora, fashioned from clay at Zeus's command. He is attended in the forge by automatons of his own making — golden handmaidens who can think and speak, and self-moving tripods, the ancient world's image of intelligent machines.

Son of [Zeus](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/zeus) and [Hera](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/hera) (or of Hera alone in some versions), Hephaestus is alone among the Olympians in being physically imperfect: lame, and cast out of heaven. His rejection sets him apart from the idealized gods, yet his hands make him indispensable. His workshop lies beneath a volcano (his Roman name, Vulcan, gives us the word). Married to [Aphrodite](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/aphrodite), the most beautiful of the goddesses, their mismatched union is mythology's great study in the tension between beauty and craft, surface and substance.

---

## Key Myths

**Cast from Olympus.** In one telling, Hera, ashamed of her imperfect son, hurled the infant Hephaestus from Olympus; he fell a full day and was crippled by the landing. In another, he intervened in a quarrel to defend his mother, and Zeus flung him from the heights. Either way, his lameness is the wound of rejection by his own parents.

**Revenge on Hera.** To repay the mother who cast him out, Hephaestus crafted a golden throne of exquisite beauty and sent it to her. When Hera sat in it, invisible bonds seized her fast, and no god could free her. Only Hephaestus knew the trick — and he refused to come, until [Dionysus](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/dionysus) got him drunk and led him back to Olympus to release her, winning his return to the company of the gods.

**The net for Ares and Aphrodite.** Learning from Helios that his wife Aphrodite was sleeping with [Ares](/knowledge/mythology/greek-mythology/ares), Hephaestus forged a net of chains finer than spider-silk and invisible to the eye, draping it over their bed. The lovers were caught fast in the act, and the smith summoned the other gods to witness their humiliation — craft outwitting both beauty and brute strength.

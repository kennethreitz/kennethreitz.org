# Hippocampus

> *The cartographer of memory — drawing maps of where you were and when it happened.*

---

## Overview

The **hippocampus** is a curved, seahorse-shaped structure — the name is Greek for seahorse — folded into the medial temporal lobe alongside the [Amygdala](/knowledge/neuroscience/amygdala), and it belongs to the [Limbic System](/knowledge/neuroscience/limbic-system). It is the brain's indexer of experience: the structure that takes the day's raw perceptions and binds them into memories that can be retrieved later. It does not store memories forever so much as it consolidates them, gradually handing the durable ones off to the cortex for long-term keeping — a slow migration that can take years, which is why old memories survive hippocampal damage while new ones cannot form. It is also the brain's mapmaker, holding a spatial model of the world. When the hippocampus is removed, as in the famous patient H.M., the capacity to form new lasting **episodic** memories is lost while old ones, moment-to-moment awareness, and the ability to learn new skills all remain — a dissociation that first revealed memory to be not one faculty but several.

## What It Does

Two functions dominate. First, **memory consolidation**: the hippocampus stitches together the scattered fragments of an event — sights, sounds, place, sequence, the feeling of it — into a coherent episode, and during deep sleep it *replays* these traces at speed, strengthening the synaptic connections and transferring the durable ones to the neocortex. That binding is glutamatergic work, resting on the long-term potentiation described under [Glutamate](/knowledge/neuroscience/glutamate): the hippocampus is one of the most plastic regions in the brain precisely because remembering *is* rewiring. Second, **spatial mapping**: it houses "place cells" that fire for specific locations — a discovery that won the 2014 Nobel Prize — an internal cartography that lets you navigate and remember where things happened. These are linked. Memory in this structure is deeply contextual, tagged with the where and when, which is why walking into an old room can flood you with the past. It is also one of the few brain regions where **neurogenesis** continues into adulthood, new neurons folded into its circuitry throughout life — a standing reserve of [Neuroplasticity](/knowledge/neuroscience/neuroplasticity) that stress and depression can suppress and that exercise and recovery can restore.

## Stress, Cortisol, and Trauma

The hippocampus is unusually vulnerable to stress. It is dense with glucocorticoid receptors, which lets it register cortisol and help switch the [HPA Axis](/knowledge/neuroscience/hpa-axis) off once a threat passes — but that same sensitivity is its weakness: chronic cortisol from sustained stress can impair its neurons, suppress its neurogenesis, and even shrink it measurably, dulling memory and weakening the very off-switch that would calm the axis, a feedback loop where stress sustains stress. This bears directly on Trauma. Under extreme threat the [Amygdala](/knowledge/neuroscience/amygdala), amplified by a [Norepinephrine](/knowledge/neuroscience/norepinephrine) surge, burns the emotional charge of an event in vividly, while the hippocampus, flooded with cortisol and impaired, fails to file it with proper time-and-place context. The result, seen in PTSD, is memory that intrudes as raw present-tense sensation rather than as something that happened *then* — a flashback that has lost its timestamp. Recovery in part involves helping the hippocampus recontextualize such memories so they can finally be dated and shelved — trauma therapies work in effect by re-activating the memory in a safe present so it can be re-encoded with new context — a process that overlaps with Healing and depends on [Neuroplasticity](/knowledge/neuroscience/neuroplasticity).

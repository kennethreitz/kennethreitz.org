# Neurotransmitters

> *The chemical words the brain whispers across the gap between one cell and the next.*

---

## Overview

A **neurotransmitter** is a chemical messenger that carries a signal across the **synapse**, the microscopic gap separating one neuron from the next. Electricity travels *within* a neuron, but it cannot leap the gap. So at the terminal the signal converts to chemistry: the cell releases packets of neurotransmitter that drift across, land on receptors on the far side, and either coax that next neuron toward firing or talk it down. This is the fundamental grammar of thought, mood, movement, and memory. The chemistry falls into rough classes, small amino acids like [Glutamate](/knowledge/neuroscience/glutamate) and GABA that do the fast, bulk signaling; the monoamines like [Dopamine](/knowledge/neuroscience/dopamine) and [Serotonin](/knowledge/neuroscience/serotonin) that modulate whole networks more slowly; and the peptides like the [Endorphins](/knowledge/neuroscience/endorphins) and [Oxytocin](/knowledge/neuroscience/oxytocin) that tune mood, pain, and bonding.

## How Transmission Works

The cycle has three beats. **Release**: an electrical impulse reaches the terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open, and vesicles fuse with the membrane to spill neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft. **Reception**: the molecules bind receptors on the receiving neuron. These come in two broad kinds, fast **ionotropic** receptors that are themselves ion channels, opening in milliseconds to nudge the cell toward (excitation) or away from (inhibition) firing, and slower **metabotropic** receptors that work through second-messenger cascades for sustained, modulatory effects. **Clearance**: the signal must end cleanly, so the sender pulls the molecules back through **reuptake** transporters or enzymes break them down in the cleft. Every step, synthesis, release, receptor binding, reuptake, breakdown, is a place where chemistry, and pharmacology, can intervene, which is exactly why the same molecule can be nudged by so many different drugs.

## The Major Players

**[Dopamine](/knowledge/neuroscience/dopamine)** governs reward, motivation, and movement; its dysregulation sits near the heart of psychosis and Parkinson's. **[Serotonin](/knowledge/neuroscience/serotonin)** modulates mood, sleep, and appetite, the target of most antidepressants. **[GABA](/knowledge/neuroscience/gaba)** is the brain's chief **inhibitory**, calming brake; too little, and anxiety and seizures follow. **[Glutamate](/knowledge/neuroscience/glutamate)** is its opposite, the dominant **excitatory** accelerator behind learning and plasticity, and GABA is literally made from it, the two forming a single balanced economy. **[Norepinephrine](/knowledge/neuroscience/norepinephrine)** drives arousal, alertness, and the stress response, and is itself synthesized from dopamine. **[Acetylcholine](/knowledge/neuroscience/acetylcholine)** runs attention and memory centrally, and muscle contraction at the periphery. Beyond these, the peptide messengers, **[endorphins](/knowledge/neuroscience/endorphins)** for pain and relief, **[oxytocin](/knowledge/neuroscience/oxytocin)** for bonding and trust, round out the family. None acts alone; mood, attention, and motivation all emerge from the interplay among them.

## Why It Matters Clinically

Nearly every psychiatric drug works by nudging this synaptic traffic, blocking a receptor, slowing reuptake, or damping release.  Antipsychotics act mainly on dopamine and serotonin receptors: some as blunt dopamine antagonists, some as partial agonists that stabilize rather than simply block. Other agents quiet overactive circuits by dampening glutamate and norepinephrine release. Understanding the messengers is understanding the dial each drug actually turns, and why side effects appear, since a drug aimed at one pathway rarely leaves the others untouched.

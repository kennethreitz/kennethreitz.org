# Acetylcholine

> *The messenger of attention — the one that decides what the moment is made of, and the one whose fading dims the whole record.*

---

## Overview

**Acetylcholine** was the first neurotransmitter ever identified — Otto Loewi's 1921 experiment slowing a frog's heart with fluid from another proved chemical transmission real — and it works two very different jobs. At the periphery it is the chemistry of **movement**: every voluntary muscle contracts because a motor neuron released acetylcholine onto it at the **neuromuscular junction**, where it acts on fast nicotinic receptors. Inside the brain, it is the chemistry of **attention and memory** — the modulator that sharpens signal against noise, marks experience as worth encoding, and keeps the cortex in a receptive, learning-ready state. It is less about the content of a thought than about *whether the brain is paying attention to it at all*. Focus, the salience of the present moment, and the laying-down of new memories all lean on cholinergic tone.

## What It Does

Acetylcholine works through two receptor families that map roughly onto its two jobs. **Nicotinic** receptors are fast ionotropic channels, dominant at muscle and quick to fire — the same receptors nicotine stimulates, which is much of why smoking sharpens attention and is so tenaciously habit-forming. **Muscarinic** receptors are slower, metabotropic, and carry most of acetylcholine's modulatory work in the brain. The signal is cleared not by reuptake but by an enzyme, **acetylcholinesterase**, which splits the molecule in the cleft almost as fast as it arrives — the target of both nerve agents and the Alzheimer's drugs discussed below.

The brain's central acetylcholine comes largely from the **basal forebrain**, whose neurons project broadly across the cortex and [Hippocampus](/knowledge/neuroscience/hippocampus) — the memory-forming structures. Cholinergic activity rises during waking attention and, notably, surges again during **REM sleep**, the dreaming phase, where it helps drive the vivid, cortically-activated internal theater of dreams while the body stays paralyzed. This dual role — alert waking and dreaming REM, both cholinergic-rich — is one of the striking symmetries of the sleeping and waking brain. Where [Norepinephrine](/knowledge/neuroscience/norepinephrine) and Serotonin fall silent in REM, acetylcholine keeps the cortex lit, and that shifted chemical balance is thought to shape the peculiar logic of dreams. Acetylcholine, in effect, gates when the cortex is turned up and listening, whether to the outer world or the inner one.

## The Alzheimer's Connection

The clearest window onto acetylcholine's role in memory is its loss. In **Alzheimer's disease**, cholinergic neurons of the basal forebrain degenerate early and severely, and the resulting collapse in cortical acetylcholine tracks closely with the failing memory and attention that define the illness. This **cholinergic hypothesis** gave the first generation of Alzheimer's drugs — the **cholinesterase inhibitors** like donepezil, which block the enzyme that breaks acetylcholine down and so prop up what remains in the synapse. They do not cure or stop the disease; they modestly, temporarily raise the level of the messenger a dying system can no longer supply. That even a partial restoration eases symptoms is itself the argument for how much of memory rides on this one molecule. The mirror image is instructive too: **anticholinergic** drugs, which block muscarinic receptors, reliably cloud memory and attention and can produce confusion in the elderly — the deficit induced on purpose. It is a caution that runs quietly under psychiatric treatment, since many older antipsychotics and sedatives carry anticholinergic effects as an unwanted cost.

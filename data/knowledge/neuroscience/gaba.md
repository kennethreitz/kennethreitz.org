# GABA

> *The brake, the quiet no that keeps every yes from becoming a seizure.*

---

## Overview

**GABA** (gamma-aminobutyric acid) is the brain's chief **inhibitory** neurotransmitter, the one that tells neurons to stand down. Where most messengers coax a cell toward firing, GABA does the opposite: it opens chloride channels, hyperpolarizes the receiving neuron, and raises the bar it must clear to fire at all. Roughly a third of the brain's synapses run on it. Without that constant damping, excitation would run away with itself, which is exactly what a seizure is. GABA is not the absence of signal; it is an active, sculpting force, carving quiet into the noise so that thought has shape.

Its origin is telling. GABA is synthesized directly from [Glutamate](/knowledge/neuroscience/glutamate), the brain's chief excitatory messenger, by the enzyme glutamate decarboxylase, the accelerator's own molecule converted, one chemical step, into the brake. The two are not separate systems so much as two faces of a single metabolic economy, which is part of why the balance between them is so tightly and locally held.

## What It Does

GABA acts mostly through two receptor families. **GABA-A** receptors are fast ionotropic channels, they open, chloride floods in, the neuron goes quiet within milliseconds. This is the target of most of pharmacology's calming agents. **GABA-B** receptors are slower, metabotropic, coupling through G-proteins and second messengers to open potassium channels for more sustained, modulatory inhibition. Together they set the brain's inhibitory tone, the background hush against which meaningful signals stand out. Beyond simply quieting, GABAergic interneurons also *sculpt timing*, the fast-spiking cells that pace cortical rhythms, including the gamma oscillations thought to bind perception into coherent moments, run on GABA. Inhibition is not only a floor on activity; it is the metronome that gives activity its rhythm.

The **excitation-inhibition balance**: GABA against glutamate: is one of the deepest organizing principles in neural function, and when it tilts too far toward excitation, the results are anxiety, insomnia, and at the extreme, convulsions. Its steady presence is also what lets the fast excitatory traffic of thought stay legible instead of collapsing into noise or seizure.

## Why Inhibition Matters for Anxiety

Insufficient GABAergic tone leaves circuits over-excitable, and the felt experience of that is often [Anxiety](/knowledge/psychology/conditions/anxiety), a nervous system that cannot quite talk itself down. This is why the drugs that most reliably quiet anxiety act here. **Benzodiazepines** (diazepam, lorazepam, clonazepam) do not open GABA-A channels themselves; they are **positive allosteric modulators**, binding a separate site and making the receptor more responsive to the brain's own GABA, so the brake bites harder wherever GABA is already being released. **Barbiturates** and the anesthetic **propofol** act on nearby sites of the same receptor, and **alcohol** does something similar, potentiating GABA-A while blunting glutamate, which is why a drink loosens and slows, and why alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal, with their rebound excitation, can be genuinely dangerous, seizures included. That a sedative-hypnotic, an anesthetic, and the world's most common intoxicant all converge on this one receptor says how central inhibition is to feeling at ease.

The [Amygdala](/knowledge/neuroscience/amygdala) carries dense GABAergic circuitry of its own, and part of what benzodiazepines quiet is the threat alarm itself, the felt "talking down" is inhibition reasserting over a limbic system running hot. Agents such as gabapentin, despite the name, do not act on GABA receptors directly. They calm overactive circuits by dampening excitatory release, arriving at a similar quiet from the other side of the balance.

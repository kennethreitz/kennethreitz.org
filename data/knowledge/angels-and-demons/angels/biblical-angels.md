# Biblical Angels

> *The messengers of scripture — before the hierarchies were drawn, there were only the ones who came and spoke.*

---

## The Angels of Scripture

Long before Pseudo-Dionysius sorted the heavenly host into nine ranks, the angels of the Bible appear as something plainer and stranger: messengers (the Hebrew *mal'akh* and the Greek *angelos* both mean simply "messenger") who arrive, deliver a word or a judgment, and depart. They are rarely described in detail and almost never named. Only a handful carry names across the canonical and deuterocanonical texts, and those names became the seed of the whole later system of [Archangels](/knowledge/angels-and-demons/angels/archangels) and choirs.

## The Named Ones

**[Michael](/knowledge/angels-and-demons/angels/michael)** — the only angel scripture calls an archangel by name (Jude 1:9), the great prince who stands guard over Israel (Daniel 10, 12) and wars against the dragon in Revelation 12. **[Gabriel](/knowledge/angels-and-demons/angels/gabriel)** — the herald who interprets Daniel's visions and announces the births of John and of Christ (Luke 1). **[Raphael](/knowledge/angels-and-demons/angels/raphael)** — who walks with Tobias and binds a demon in the Book of Tobit, naming himself "one of the seven who stand before the Lord." **[Uriel](/knowledge/angels-and-demons/angels/uriel)** — not in the Protestant canon but prominent in the Book of Enoch and 2 Esdras, the angel of light and of the questions that have no easy answer.

## The Living Creatures

The strangest angels are the ones scripture *does* describe. The **[Seraphim](/knowledge/angels-and-demons/angels/seraphim)** ("burning ones") appear once, in Isaiah 6 — six-winged, crying *Holy, holy, holy* around the throne. The **[Cherubim](/knowledge/angels-and-demons/angels/cherubim)** guard Eden with a flaming sword (Genesis 3), overshadow the Ark, and reappear in Ezekiel's vision as the four-faced, many-eyed *hayyot*, the living creatures beneath the chariot-throne. John's Revelation gathers them again as the four beasts around the throne. These are the raw material the tradition later ranked and tamed.

## The Angel of the Lord

A recurring and ambiguous figure — the *mal'akh YHWH* who speaks to Hagar, stays Abraham's knife, wrestles Jacob, and burns in the bush before Moses — often speaking as God in the first person. Whether a distinct angel or a veiled appearance of the divine itself is a question the text leaves open.

# Uriel

> *“God is my light” — the fire held low, that even the earth might see.*

---

## The Archangel

Uriel is the angel of illumination, the bearer of the flame of divine wisdom, and among the archangels the most ambiguous and the most earthbound. His name means “God is my light,” and in the older apocalyptic literature he is the interpreter of mysteries, the one who leads the seer through the hidden chambers of creation and explains the movements of the luminaries. He is called the angel of repentance and of the final reckoning, standing at the gate with a fiery sword after the expulsion from Eden — and yet also the one who tends the buried seed, the guardian of the underworld’s thresholds and of the slow alchemy of the soil. Where the other princes turn their faces to sun, moon and star, Uriel turns his downward, toward the dark and patient ground, and finds the light of God even there.

His element is earth — and in some systems the cold outer fire of Uranus, the planet of sudden awakening — and this earthiness is the key to his paradox. The flame he carries is not the leaping fire of the warrior nor the reflected silver of the moon, but the banked ember, the coal that warms the root through winter. He governs illumination that comes not as a flash but as a slow dawning, the repentance that turns a life around by degrees, the wisdom quarried out of hard experience. Iconographically he is robed in olive and citrine, in russet and black, an open scroll or a flame cupped in his palm, his gaze steady and grave. He assigns himself no fixed station on the great scheme, standing rather at the northern gate, keeper of the midnight from which all mornings come.

## Scripture and Tradition

Uriel is not named in the canonical Bible, and this absence is itself part of his character: he is the archangel of the hidden and the apocryphal, glimpsed at the edges of the visible tradition. His great text is the Second Book of Esdras, where he is sent to instruct the prophet Ezra, answering his anguished questions about the justice of God with riddles of his own — “weigh me the weight of the fire, or measure me the blast of the wind” — teaching that the ways of the Most High are not to be measured by mortal reckoning. In the Book of Enoch he is one of the great watchers, the angel set over the luminaries and the ordering of the heavens, who shows Enoch the courses of the sun and moon and the places of the winds. Later legend makes him the angel who warned Noah of the coming flood, the flaming sentinel posted at the gate of Eden, and one of the four who stand about the throne, guarding the northern quarter. The Church long venerated him among the archangels until his name was quietly set aside, which only deepened his association with the buried, the unofficial, and the wisdom that survives underground.

Unlike his brothers, Uriel is not fixed to a single sphere on the Tree of Life; the tradition leaves his station open, and this rootlessness suits the angel of the earth and the underworld, who moves through all the lower gates. His direction is the North, the place of midnight, cold, and the unseen, and his element is earth — though some esoteric systems assign him the abrupt, awakening fire of Uranus, the lightning that illuminates without warning. His colours are olive, russet, citrine, and black — the hues of autumn soil and banked coals — and his emblems the open book, the scroll, and the flame held low in an open hand. To invoke Uriel is to call upon the light that dawns slowly, the wisdom gathered from hard ground, and the repentance that turns a whole life by patient degrees.

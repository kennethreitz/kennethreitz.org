# Seraphim

> *They burn, and are not consumed.*

---

## The Choir

The Seraphim are the highest choir of the celestial hierarchy, the angels who stand nearest to the divine presence and are, in the vision of Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, the very embodiment of love in its most incandescent form. Their name derives from the Hebrew *saraph*, “to burn,” and it is fire — pure, ceaseless, unquenchable — that defines them. The same root names the “fiery serpents” of the wilderness in Numbers and the bronze *seraph* Moses raised on a pole, so that heat, brightness, and a certain serpentine swiftness cling to the word from the beginning. They do not merely serve the flame of God; they are that flame, caught up in an eternal ecstasy of adoration so intense that it purifies everything it touches.

Their sole scriptural appearance by name is the sixth chapter of Isaiah, where the prophet beholds them above the throne, each with six wings: two to cover the face, two to cover the feet, and two with which to fly. They call ceaselessly to one another, “Holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts,” and the posts of the door shake at the voice while the house fills with smoke. From this vision the Church drew the *Sanctus* of the liturgy, so that every celebration of the Eucharist joins the burning song of the highest angels. When Isaiah confesses his unclean lips, it is a seraph who takes a live coal from the altar and touches his mouth — the choir of fire performing, once and unforgettably, an act of purification upon a man.

To the Seraphim falls no task of governance, no message to carry, no nation to guard. Their entire office is contemplation and praise, and in this they set the pattern for all lower ranks. The heat of their love, the tradition holds, flows downward through the hierarchy, kindling wisdom in the Cherubim and illumination in the choirs below. They are closest to the source and therefore least intelligible to human understanding — creatures not of intellect first but of ardor, whose knowledge of God is inseparable from their burning for God. Where the Cherubim know, the Seraphim love, and in the economy of heaven that love is the higher and the hotter thing.

## In the Tradition

Pseudo-Dionysius, writing in the late fifth or early sixth century, placed the Seraphim at the summit of his *Celestial Hierarchy* and read their very name as a theology: they are the “burning ones” whose perpetual motion around the divine is an unquenchable, elevating heat that consumes all shadow and casts light upon those below. Thomas Aquinas, taking up the scheme in the *Summa*, argued that the orders are named for their gifts, and that the Seraphim signify the excess of charity — the love that unites most closely to God — while conceding that in another respect the Cherubim, named for knowledge, might be reckoned their equals. Bonaventure and the mystics made the six-winged seraph an emblem of ascent, and it was in the form of a crucified seraph that Francis of Assisi received the stigmata on Mount Alverna, binding the choir of fire forever to the wounds of Christ. In art they are rendered red or aflame, faces veiled, wings many-eyed, always in motion — the color of love that does not cool.

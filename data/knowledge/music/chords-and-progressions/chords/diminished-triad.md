# Diminished Triad

> *A held breath that refuses to become a sigh.*

---

## The Chord

The diminished triad is what happens when you take the minor triad and squeeze it. Both the third and the fifth are lowered, so instead of stacking a minor third and then a major third, you stack two minor thirds — three equal steps that compress the chord into something taut and narrow. On C the notes are C, E♭, and G♭. That flattened fifth, the tritone away from the root, is the source of all the trouble.

A diminished triad has no perfect fifth to steady it, and without that anchor the chord seems to lean. The two minor thirds are symmetrical enough to feel unmoored, yet the tritone between root and fifth is one of the most directional intervals in music. The chord wants to go somewhere, urgently.

## Structure

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Formula | 1 ♭3 ♭5 |
| Notes (on C) | C E♭ G♭ |
| Symbol | C° (Cdim) |
| Quality | Tense, unstable |

## Character

The diminished triad is pure unrest. It sounds like a question, a threat, a moment of vertigo. Because its tritone demands resolution, the chord rarely feels like a destination; it feels like a corner being turned. In small doses it colours a phrase with anxiety or drama, and its instability is precisely why it never overstays.

## In Practice

The diminished triad appears diatonically as the vii° of a major key — the leading-tone chord that strains upward toward the tonic. It functions as a dominant substitute, carrying the same tritone tension that pulls a phrase home. Passing diminished chords link two stable harmonies with a moment of chromatic motion, a trick as common in ragtime as in film scoring. Extend it and it becomes the still darker [Diminished Seventh](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/chords/diminished-seventh).

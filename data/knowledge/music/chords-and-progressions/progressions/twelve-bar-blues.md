# Twelve-Bar Blues

> *Twelve bars wide, and deep enough to fall into.*

---

## The Progression

The twelve-bar blues is less a chord progression than a form — a repeating twelve-measure frame built from the three primary chords of a key. The most common shape gives four bars of the tonic, two of the subdominant, two more of the tonic, then a turnaround through the dominant and back. Over this scaffold the melody, the lyric, and the improvised solo unfold in a call-and-response arc: a line, its repetition, and a resolving answer.

Its emotional charge comes from the dominant sevenths. Blues players typically color all three chords as sevenths, so even the "home" tonic carries the restless bite of a flatted seventh. The tension never fully dissolves — it just cycles, which mirrors the form's lyrical logic of stating a trouble, restating it, and answering it. The predictable frame is exactly what frees the soloist to roam.

## Structure

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Roman numerals | I–I–I–I / IV–IV–I–I / V–IV–I–V |
| Example in C | C7 (×4) / F7 – F7 – C7 – C7 / G7 – F7 – C7 – G7 |
| Chords used | [Dominant Seventh](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/chords/dominant-seventh) (I, IV, V) |
| Character | Earthy, cyclical, expressive |
| Function | 12-bar frame: tonic → subdominant → dominant turnaround |

## In Practice

It is the foundation of blues and the direct ancestor of rock and roll — "Sweet Home Chicago," "Johnny B. Goode," "Hound Dog," and "Rock Around the Clock" all ride the twelve-bar frame. Countless variations exist, from the quick-change (moving to IV in bar two) to jazz-blues reharmonizations that lace [ii-V-I](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/ii-v-i) cadences into the turnaround. At heart it remains the [I-IV-V](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/i-iv-v) stretched across twelve measures.

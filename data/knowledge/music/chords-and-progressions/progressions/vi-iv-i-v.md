# vi-IV-I-V

> *The same four chords, but they start you in the dark.*

---

## The Progression

The vi–IV–I–V is the melancholy sibling of the pop axis. It contains exactly the same chords as the [I-V-vi-IV](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/i-v-vi-iv) loop, but by beginning on the relative minor it reframes the whole cycle: the ear hears the vi as home, and the progression reads as a minor-key lament that keeps reaching toward, but never quite settling into, its major relative. The mood tilts immediately toward sorrow, resignation, or brooding intensity.

Because it starts in shadow, every subsequent chord feels like an attempt to climb out of it. The IV and I offer glimpses of light, but the closing V yanks the loop back to the top and the minor tonic reasserts itself. That perpetual, unresolved striving is what gives the progression its emotional pull — it sounds like longing that never gets what it wants.

## Structure

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Roman numerals | vi – IV – I – V |
| Example in C | Am – F – C – G |
| Chords used | [Minor Triad](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/chords/minor-triad) (vi), [Major Triad](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/chords/major-triad) (IV, I, V) |
| Character | Melancholy, yearning, driving |
| Function | Relative minor → Subdominant → Tonic → Dominant |

## In Practice

It saturates emotional pop, rock ballads, and film music — "Zombie," "Numb," "Save Tonight," and "Africa" all lean on this darker rotation. Songwriters reach for it when a lyric needs weight or vulnerability, exploiting the fact that listeners recognize the axis chords while feeling an entirely different mood. It is a direct rotation of the [I-V-vi-IV](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/i-v-vi-iv) and shares its family with the [Fifties Progression](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/fifties-progression).

# I-IV-V

> *Three chords and the truth.*

---

## The Progression

The I–IV–V is the load-bearing wall of Western popular music. Built entirely from the three major triads that occur naturally in a major key, it uses only the tonic, the subdominant, and the dominant — the three chords whose roots sit a fifth apart and whose combined notes cover every degree of the scale. Play them in sequence and you have narrated the whole tonal story: home, departure, tension, and the pull back home again.

What makes it work is the strength of the fifth-relationships. The IV pulls gently away from the tonic while the V, containing the leading tone, strains hard to resolve back to I. That V–I motion is the most decisive gesture in tonal harmony, and the IV softens the setup so the return lands with weight rather than surprise. Nothing is wasted; nothing is ambiguous.

## Structure

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Roman numerals | I – IV – V |
| Example in C | C – F – G |
| Chords used | [Major Triad](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/chords/major-triad) (all three) |
| Character | Bright, resolute, foundational |
| Function | Tonic → Subdominant → Dominant |

## In Practice

This is the three-chord backbone of blues, folk, rock, and country — the progression a beginner learns first and a master never abandons. It underpins countless standards: "La Bamba," "Twist and Shout," "Wild Thing," "Johnny B. Goode," and an entire lineage of rock and roll that grew directly out of the [Twelve-Bar Blues](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/twelve-bar-blues), which is itself just I, IV, and V arranged over twelve measures. Its universality is precisely the point — a shared vocabulary that lets a whole room play along.

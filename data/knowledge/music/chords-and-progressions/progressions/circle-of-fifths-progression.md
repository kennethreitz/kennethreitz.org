# Circle of Fifths Progression

> *Roots falling like water, always downhill toward home.*

---

## The Progression

The circle of fifths progression chains chords whose roots descend by perfect fifths, each one leaning into the next the way a dominant leans into its tonic. The classic short form — vi–ii–V–I — is a fragment of this larger wheel, and it can be extended backward through iii and beyond to circle almost the entire diatonic set. Every step is the strongest root motion in tonal harmony, so the whole sequence feels propelled, gravitationally certain of where it is going.

What the ear registers is momentum. Because each chord acts momentarily as a dominant to the one that follows, the progression generates a cascade of small resolutions that only fully settle when it reaches the tonic. Voice-leading falls naturally into place, guide tones sliding by step, which is why this motion sounds equally at home in a Bach chorale and a jazz turnaround.

| Attribute | Value |

| Character | Flowing, inevitable, propulsive |
| Function | Descending-fifths motion resolving to tonic |

## In Practice

It underpins both jazz and classical harmonic motion — the bridge of "rhythm changes," countless Baroque sequences, and standards like "Autumn Leaves" and "I Will Survive" ride waves of descending fifths. Its final three chords are exactly the [ii-V-I](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/ii-v-i), and the [Backdoor Progression](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/backdoor-progression) offers an alternate route to the same tonic destination.

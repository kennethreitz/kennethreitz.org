# Backdoor Progression

> *You expected the front door. It comes in through the back.*

---

## The Progression

The backdoor progression is a jazz substitute cadence: ii–♭VII7–I, where a dominant seventh built on the flatted seventh degree stands in for the usual V. In C the expected G7 is replaced by B♭7, and that chord resolves up to Cmaj7 not through a leading-tone pull but through a smooth, unexpected side entrance — hence the name. The ♭VII7 shares tones with the tonic that make its arrival feel warm and inevitable rather than jarring.

The trick lies in voice-leading. B♭7 contains the notes A♭ and D; as it resolves to Cmaj7, A♭ falls to G and D holds or rises to E, gliding the ear home from an angle it did not anticipate. It borrows the flavor of the IV minor world — the ♭VII7 functions like the dominant of a borrowed subdominant — giving the resolution a soulful, gospel-tinged softness that a straight V–I cannot.

## Structure

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Roman numerals | ii – ♭VII7 – I |
| Example in C | Dm7 – B♭7 – Cmaj7 |
| Chords used | [Minor Seventh](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/chords/minor-seventh) (ii), [Dominant Seventh](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/chords/dominant-seventh) (♭VII7), [Major Seventh](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/chords/major-seventh) (I) |
| Character | Warm, unexpected, soulful |
| Function | Predominant → backdoor dominant → tonic |

## In Practice

It colors jazz, soul, and gospel harmony — Steely Dan, Stevie Wonder, and countless standards slip through this backdoor to soften a turnaround or dodge a predictable cadence. Arrangers reach for it when a plain [ii-V-I](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/ii-v-i) feels too direct and a bit of shadow is wanted. It sits within the broader logic of the [Circle of Fifths Progression](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/circle-of-fifths-progression) and the substitute [Cadences](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/cadences).

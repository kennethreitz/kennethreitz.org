# I-V-vi-IV

> *Four chords, a thousand songs.*

---

## The Progression

The I–V–vi–IV is the most famous chord loop in modern pop — the so-called "four chords" that a comedy act can string a medley of dozens of hit songs through without changing a single shape. It moves from the tonic to the dominant, drops to the relative minor, and lands on the subdominant before circling back to the top. The result is a loop that feels both uplifting and quietly emotional, endlessly repeatable without fatigue.

Its genius lies in the vi. Where a plain I–V–IV would stay relentlessly bright, the swing through the relative minor introduces a shadow — a moment of longing — before the IV lifts the phrase back toward resolution. The progression never fully cadences; it just keeps turning, which is exactly why it works so well under a chorus meant to be sung again and again.

| Character | Anthemic, warm, bittersweet-optimistic |
| Function | Tonic → Dominant → Relative minor → Subdominant |

## In Practice

Nicknamed the "axis" progression, it powers an astonishing share of chart music across pop, rock, and country: "Let It Be," "With or Without You," "Don't Stop Believin'," and "Someone Like You" all lean on it. Its four rotations — starting the loop on a different chord each time — yield the [vi-IV-I-V](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/vi-iv-i-v) and the [Fifties Progression](/knowledge/music/chords-and-progressions/progressions/fifties-progression) among others, a family of interrelated cycles built from the same four chords.

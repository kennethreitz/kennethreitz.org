# Single Dragadiddle

> *A drag on the downbeat, a diddle to finish.*

---

## The Rudiment

The single dragadiddle grafts a drag onto a paradiddle-style figure. The cell opens with an accented, dragged stroke and closes with a double, so it reads as “drag-TAP, tap-tap” — the ruff giving weight to the front of the pattern and the diddle rounding out the back. Like the flam paradiddle it takes a familiar sticking shape and ornaments the leading note, but here the ornament is a two-grace-note drag rather than a single-grace flam.

## Sticking

The single dragadiddle is conventionally written as a paradiddle-length figure with a drag on the first note: **llRLRR** for the first pass and **rrLRLL** for the second. The two lowercase strokes are the drag’s grace notes; the accented primary opens the figure, followed by a single and then a double that sets up the opposite hand. Counted in sixteenths, the dragged accent lands on the beat and the closing diddle fills the second half of the group. Because printed versions vary, lean on the shape — dragged accent, then the single-and-double of a paradiddle.

## How to Play

Play the drag as a low, crisp, controlled double, pop the accent on the primary, then let the paradiddle unwind at a lower dynamic with a relaxed, rebound-driven diddle. The coordination challenge is launching cleanly from a drag into a flowing sticking pattern without the ruff dragging the tempo. Keep the grace notes quiet and even, and let the diddle ride out so it hands off smoothly to the next drag.

## In Practice

The single dragadiddle appears in rudimental solos as an ornamented, driving sixteenth figure, and on the drum set it makes a punchy fill — the drag buzzing into an accented drum, the diddle spilling onto another. It’s a natural next step for anyone comfortable with both the paradiddle and the basic drag, joining the two vocabularies into one flowing pattern.

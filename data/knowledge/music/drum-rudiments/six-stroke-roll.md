# Six Stroke Roll

> *Accents on the outside, diddles in the middle — a groove hiding in a roll.*

---

## The Rudiment

The six stroke roll is the odd one out among the numbered rolls: rather than resolving a run of diddles onto a single accent, it brackets two doubles between two accented single strokes. The result is a self-contained six-note cell — accent, diddle, diddle, accent — that loops beautifully and has become a modern drum set staple. Its symmetry and built-in accents make it as much a groove tool as a rudiment, especially when the accents are moved around the kit.

## Sticking

The common sticking places accented singles on the outside and doubles inside: **R LL RR L**, with accents on the first and last notes, then reversed as **L RR LL R**. There are several accepted accent placements for the six stroke roll, but the outer-accent version is the one most players reach for. Counted as a sextuplet across one beat, the pattern locks neatly into the pulse and repeats hand to hand.

## How to Play

Play the two singles as full accented strokes and keep the inner doubles low and even, so the cell breathes as loud-soft-soft-soft-soft-loud. On the kit it comes alive when you orchestrate the accents — moving the singles to toms or cymbals while the diddles stay on the snare. Keep the fulcrum loose so the doubles rebound cleanly between the two accents.

## In Practice

The six stroke roll is a favorite for modern grooves and fills, jazz and fusion phrasing, and any place a repeating accented roll cell fits. It sits among the [Five Stroke Roll](/knowledge/music/drum-rudiments/five-stroke-roll) and [Seven Stroke Roll](/knowledge/music/drum-rudiments/seven-stroke-roll) but behaves more like a looping figure than a terminating roll.

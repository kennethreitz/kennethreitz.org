# Chromatic Scale

> *Every door in the building, all at once.*

---

## The Scale

The chromatic scale is not really a scale in the ordinary sense — it is the full inventory of pitches from which all other scales are carved. It contains all twelve tones of Western equal temperament, each a half step from the next, dividing the octave into its smallest standard increments. Where a diatonic scale is a selection, a set of choices about which notes belong, the chromatic scale makes no choices at all. Its name comes from the Greek *chroma*, colour, and the metaphor is apt: if the diatonic scale is the drawing, the chromatic notes are the full palette from which any colour can be drawn.

## Structure

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Notes (from C) | C C♯ D D♯ E F F♯ G G♯ A A♯ B |
| Interval formula | All half steps |
| Number of notes | 12 |
| Defining feature | Every pitch; pure colour and passing motion |

## Character

Because every interval is identical, the chromatic scale has no tonal centre and no internal hierarchy — no note pulls harder than any other, so it cannot establish a key on its own. Played straight, it sounds like motion without destination, a smooth glide or a nervous run. Its power is not as a melody but as a source of tension and colour: a chromatic passing tone slipped between two diatonic notes adds friction and forward momentum that the plain scale degrees cannot supply.

## In Practice

In tonal music the chromatic scale mostly appears in fragments — passing tones, chromatic runs, sighing appoggiaturas that decorate an otherwise diatonic line. Composers from the Romantics onward pushed it further, until the twentieth century made it structural: serialism and twelve-tone technique treat all twelve notes as equals. In jazz and film scoring, chromatic movement supplies unease, glamour, and the sense of a harmony sliding out from under your feet.

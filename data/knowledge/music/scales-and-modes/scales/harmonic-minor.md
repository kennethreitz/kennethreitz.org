# Harmonic Minor

> *The minor scale with a knife hidden in its seventh.*

---

## The Scale

The harmonic minor is what happens when a composer refuses to give up the pull of a leading tone. Take the natural minor — the [Aeolian](/knowledge/music/scales-and-modes/modes/aeolian) mode — and raise its seventh degree by a half step, and you get a scale that resolves home with the same gravitational urgency as the major scale, while keeping the darkness of a flat third. The name is not decorative: the raised seventh exists to make the dominant chord *major*, so that harmony in a minor key can cadence with real finality. It emerged in Western practice precisely because functional harmony demanded a leading tone that natural minor simply could not supply.

## Structure

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Notes (from C) | C D E♭ F G A♭ B |
| Interval formula | W H W W H (W+H) H |
| Number of notes | 7 |
| Defining feature | Natural minor with a raised 7th; the augmented-second gap gives an exotic, Middle-Eastern colour |

## Character

The signature of the harmonic minor lives in a single interval — the augmented second between the flat sixth and the raised seventh. That gap of three half steps is wider than any step in a diatonic scale, and the ear hears it immediately as something foreign, ceremonial, faintly menacing. It is the sound Western listeners reach for when they want to evoke the East, the mystical, the old and the sacred. Underneath that exotic surface, though, sits genuine harmonic muscle: this is the scale that lets minor keys speak with authority.

## In Practice

You hear it everywhere its augmented second can do dramatic work — in flamenco and Middle-Eastern music, in the classical minor-key repertoire from Bach through Rachmaninoff, and in neoclassical metal, where guitarists mine it for velocity and menace in equal measure. It also underpins the harmony of countless minor-key songs whose melodies never touch the raised seventh at all; the note is doing its job in the chords.

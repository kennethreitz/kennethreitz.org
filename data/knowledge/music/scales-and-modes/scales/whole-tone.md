# Whole Tone

> *A staircase where every step is the same height, leading nowhere in particular.*

---

## The Scale

The whole tone scale is built from a single, relentless rule: every interval is a whole step. Six notes climb the octave in equal strides, with no half steps anywhere to create the tension and resolution that ordinary scales depend on. Because the octave divides evenly into six whole tones, there are only two distinct whole-tone scales in all of music — start on any of the twelve pitches and you land in one of two collections. This perfect symmetry is the scale's entire identity. It has no tonic, no leading tone, no natural home; it is the same in every direction, like a mirror facing a mirror.

## Structure

| Attribute | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Notes (from C) | C D E F♯ G♯ A♯ |
| Interval formula | All whole steps |
| Number of notes | 6 |
| Defining feature | Symmetrical, no tonal centre; the dreamy, weightless Debussy sound |

## Character

With no half steps to pull the ear toward a resting point, the whole tone scale floats. It sounds weightless, dreamlike, faintly disorienting — a harmony with the ground removed. Every chord built from it is some flavour of augmented, and augmented chords share that same rootless ambiguity, so the scale's melodies and its harmonies reinforce each other's suspension. It is beautiful and slightly uncanny, the musical equivalent of a slow fade or a dissolving image.

## In Practice

Its great champion was Debussy, who used it to conjure mist, water, and reverie in the Impressionist style, and it remains shorthand for that hazy, shimmering colour. Film and television lean on it for dream sequences and hallucinations, precisely because it refuses to feel solid. In jazz it appears over augmented and altered dominant chords, where its symmetry supplies a bright, unmoored tension.

# Lydian

> *Major, but hovering an inch off the ground.*

---

## The Mode

Lydian is the fourth mode of the major scale, starting on its fourth degree. Built on C it borrows from G major, which raises a single note — the fourth — by a half step. Everything else matches the ordinary major scale, so Lydian is essentially Ionian with one bright, floating alteration, and that alteration changes everything.

The raised fourth lends the mode a weightless, dreamlike, almost celestial quality. It sounds like major but somehow more so — lifted, luminous, faintly otherworldly. Many composers describe Lydian as the sound of wonder or awe, major-key brightness pushed toward the ethereal.

## Structure

| Attribute | Value |
|---|---|
| Interval formula | W W W H W W H |
| Notes (from C) | C D E F♯ G A B |
| Characteristic tone | The ♯4th — floating, dreamlike |
| Parent major scale | G major |

## Character

The sharpened fourth is what gives Lydian its shimmer. In the plain major scale the fourth wants to pull down toward the third; raise it and that gravity releases, leaving the note suspended and bright. The tritone it forms with the root refuses to resolve within the mode, so the whole scale seems to float without touching down. It is brightness without the homey grounding of Ionian — light that hovers rather than settles.

## In Practice

Lydian is a favourite of film composers reaching for magic, space, or open sky — its glow underpins countless scores of wonder and adventure. Jazz musicians prize it over major chords for the same lift, and it colours plenty of progressive rock and dreamy pop. Where a scene needs to feel like looking upward, Lydian tends to be doing the work.

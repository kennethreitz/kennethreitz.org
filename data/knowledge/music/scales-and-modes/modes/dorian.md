# Dorian

> *A minor scale that keeps one window open to the light.*

---

## The Mode

Dorian is the second mode of the major scale — start on the second degree and run to its octave. Built on C, it borrows its notes from B♭ major, giving a minor-sounding scale with a flattened third and seventh but, crucially, a natural sixth left intact. That single unflattened note is what separates Dorian from the plainer natural minor.

The result is a mode that is unmistakably minor in temperament yet refuses to be gloomy. It has a wistful, hopeful quality — melancholy with the collar turned up against the cold, a sadness that still looks outward. Among the minor modes it is the most balanced and the most cool-headed.

## Structure

| Attribute | Value |
|---|---|
| Interval formula | W H W W W H W |
| Notes (from C) | C D E♭ F G A B♭ |
| Characteristic tone | The natural 6th — a bright note inside a minor scale |
| Parent major scale | B♭ major |

## Character

Everything in Dorian turns on that natural sixth. In a minor context the ear expects the sixth to be lowered, so hearing it raised lends a lift — a splash of brightness against the minor third’s shadow. It keeps the mode from settling into pure sorrow, producing instead something poised and bittersweet, sophisticated rather than mournful. Symmetry helps too: the scale reads the same forwards and backwards, which gives it an even, self-contained balance.

## In Practice

Dorian is the workhorse of modal jazz, funk, and rock — think extended vamps that stay on one minor chord and let the natural sixth glow. It colours a great deal of Celtic and English folk song, medieval church music, and countless minor-key grooves that need to breathe rather than mourn. It is arguably the most-used mode after the two that define major and minor themselves.

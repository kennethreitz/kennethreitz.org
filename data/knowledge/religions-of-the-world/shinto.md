# Shinto

> *Even in a single leaf of a tree, or a tender blade of grass, the awe-inspiring Deity manifests itself.*

---

## Overview

Shinto — "the way of the **kami**" — is the indigenous religious tradition of Japan: not a founded religion with a prophet and a creed, but the accumulated practice of a people living among spirits. It has no founder, no central scripture, no required beliefs, and historically no name; it only needed one when [Buddhism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/buddhism) arrived in the 6th century and the older way required something to be called. Shinto is less a system of doctrine than a sensibility: the world is alive, presence is everywhere, purity matters, and gratitude is the correct posture toward a landscape full of gods.

---

## Origins and History

Shinto's roots reach into Japan's prehistory — animist and agricultural practices of the Yayoi period and earlier, rites for rice, rain, ancestors, and the spirits of notable places. Its mythology was first written down in the 8th century, when the imperial court commissioned the *Kojiki* (712) and *Nihon Shoki* (720) to record the age of the gods and, not incidentally, to trace the imperial line to the sun goddess **Amaterasu**.

For most of its history Shinto did not stand apart from Buddhism. From the Nara period onward the two braided together into **shinbutsu-shūgō**: kami were understood as local manifestations of buddhas and bodhisattvas, shrines and temples shared grounds and clergy, and ordinary people saw no contradiction in honoring both. This syncretism lasted a thousand years, until the Meiji government forcibly separated the traditions in 1868 and constructed **State Shinto** — an emperor-centered civic cult that was harnessed to nationalism and dismantled by the Allied occupation in 1945. Postwar Shinto returned to what it had mostly always been: shrines, festivals, and the quiet rituals of everyday life. Today the majority of Japanese people participate in Shinto practice while few would name it as their "religion" — a distinction that says more about the Western category than about Shinto.

## Core Beliefs

The heart of Shinto is the **kami** — a word that resists translation. Kami are gods, spirits, presences, the sacred quality in things: Amaterasu in the sun, Inari of rice and prosperity, the spirit of a particular mountain, a great tree, a waterfall, an extraordinary ancestor. The classical scholar Motoori Norinaga defined kami as whatever possesses a quality that inspires awe. Tradition speaks of *yaoyorozu no kami* — the eight million kami — a number meaning simply "beyond counting." Kami are not omnipotent or wholly other; they are powerful, local, responsive, and part of the same world as humans, capable of blessing and of anger.

The governing moral axis is not sin and salvation but **purity and pollution**. **Kegare** — impurity contracted through death, blood, illness, or wrongdoing — is not guilt; it is something more like contamination, and it is dealt with not by penance but by **harae**, purification. Sincerity of heart (**makoto**) and gratitude matter more than doctrine. Shinto has remarkably little to say about the afterlife or the origin of evil; its attention is fixed on this life, this harvest, this community, this year — which is precisely why it could coexist so comfortably with Buddhism, which had the death side of things covered.

## Practices

Shinto practice centers on the **shrine** (*jinja*), marked off from ordinary space by the **torii** gate. The visit follows a simple choreography: rinse hands and mouth at the water basin (purification in miniature), approach the hall, offer a coin, bow twice, clap twice to greet the kami, pray, bow once more. Priests (*kannushi*) and shrine maidens (*miko*) maintain the rites; households keep small altars called **kamidana**.

The great communal expressions are the **matsuri** — shrine festivals in which the kami is feted and paraded through the neighborhood in a portable shrine (*mikoshi*) carried by shouting crowds: religion as celebration, exuberant and unsolemn. Life-cycle rites belong largely to Shinto — newborn blessings, the Shichi-Go-San festival for children of three, five, and seven, weddings, groundbreakings for new buildings — while funerals belong almost entirely to Buddhism. The common summary is that Japanese are born Shinto and die Buddhist. Amulets (*omamori*), fortunes (*omikuji*), votive tablets (*ema*), and the first shrine visit of the new year (*hatsumōde*, drawing tens of millions annually) keep the tradition woven through ordinary life.

---

## Sacred Texts

Shinto has no scripture in the sense that the Bible or Quran are scripture — no revealed text that defines belief. The **Kojiki** and **Nihon Shoki** preserve the mythology: the creation of the islands by Izanagi and Izanami, Izanagi's purification after visiting the land of the dead (the mythic charter for harae), Amaterasu's retreat into the rock cave and the laughter that drew her out, and the descent of her grandson to rule the earth. The 10th-century **Engishiki** preserves the ancient liturgical prayers (*norito*). These texts are treasured and studied, but practice, not reading, is the tradition's living core.

## Branches and Forms

| Form | Character |
|------|-----------|
| **Shrine Shinto** (Jinja Shinto) | The mainstream: the network of ~80,000 shrines, most affiliated with the Association of Shinto Shrines |
| **Imperial Shinto** | Rites of the imperial household, centered on Amaterasu at Ise |
| **Folk Shinto** | Uncodified local practice — roadside kami, household rites, agricultural customs |
| **Sect Shinto** (Kyōha Shinto) | Meiji-era organized movements with founders and doctrines, such as Tenrikyō and Konkōkyō |
| **State Shinto** | The 1868–1945 nationalized civic cult; dissolved after the war |

The grand shrine of Amaterasu at **Ise** is ritually rebuilt from scratch every twenty years — a practice running since the 7th century, in which impermanence and renewal are built into the architecture itself.

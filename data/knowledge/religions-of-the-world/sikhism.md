# Sikhism

> *"There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim" — a faith born in the space between two worlds, refusing to be either and honoring both*

---

## Origins and History

Sikhism began in the Punjab in the late fifteenth century, a land where [Hinduism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/hinduism) and [Islam](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/islam) lived side by side under Mughal rule — sometimes in synthesis, sometimes in tension. Guru Nanak (1469–1539), born to a Hindu family near Lahore, disappeared into a river at around age thirty and emerged three days later with a declaration that set the tradition's course: "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim." What mattered was not the label but the reality behind both — one God, beyond form and sect, to be found not through ritual, pilgrimage, or renunciation but through remembrance, honest work, and service. Nanak spent decades traveling, by tradition as far as Mecca and Tibet, singing his teachings in the company of a Muslim musician, then settled at Kartarpur to model the life he preached: a householder's life of farming, communal meals, and devotion.

Nine Gurus followed him, each developing the community. Guru Arjan, the fifth, compiled the scripture and built the Harmandir Sahib — the Golden Temple at Amritsar — with four doors open to all directions and all people; his execution by the Mughal emperor Jahangir in 1606 was the tradition's first martyrdom and its turning point. The ninth Guru, Tegh Bahadur, was executed for defending the religious freedom of Hindus he did not himself agree with — a martyrdom for someone else's faith. His son Gobind Singh, the tenth Guru, founded the Khalsa in 1699, a community of initiated saint-soldiers, and before his death in 1708 ended the line of human Gurus by conferring the guruship permanently on the scripture itself. Sikhs today number some twenty-five to thirty million — the world's fifth-largest organized religion — concentrated in Punjab with a large global diaspora.

## Core Beliefs

The tradition's creed is compressed into the Mul Mantar, the opening of the scripture: Ik Onkar — One reality, One God — whose name is Truth, the creator, without fear, without enmity, timeless in form, beyond birth, self-existent, known by the Guru's grace. Sikh theology is strictly monotheistic and rejects idols and incarnations, which aligns it with [Islam](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/islam); yet it retains [Karma](/knowledge/spirituality/karma) and rebirth from its Indic soil, which aligns it with [Hinduism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/hinduism). It is neither a blend nor a compromise but a distinct revelation that answers both.

The human problem, in Sikh terms, is haumai — self-centeredness, the ego that experiences itself as separate from God and acts accordingly through the five thieves: lust, anger, greed, attachment, and pride. The one ruled by ego is manmukh, self-facing; the goal is to become gurmukh, Guru-facing, aligned with the divine order (hukam). Liberation is union with God, achievable within ordinary life — Sikhism pointedly rejects asceticism and monasticism. Nanak's three pillars say it plainly: Naam Japna, keep the divine Name in remembrance; Kirat Karni, earn an honest living; Vand Chhakna, share what you earn. Equality is not an aspiration but a doctrine — the tradition rejected caste from the first, affirmed the full participation of women, and gave all initiated men the name Singh (lion) and women Kaur (princess) partly to erase caste surnames.

## Practices

Daily Sikh practice centers on nitnem, appointed prayers recited in the morning, evening, and before sleep, and on simran — meditative remembrance of the Name, the Sikh counterpart to what other traditions pursue through [Meditation](/knowledge/spirituality/meditation). Communal life centers on the gurdwara, where worship consists largely of kirtan, the singing of scripture set to music, in the presence of the Guru Granth Sahib enthroned as living Guru.

Every gurdwara runs a langar — a free community kitchen where all sit on the floor in rows, regardless of religion, caste, gender, or status, and eat the same simple vegetarian meal. Instituted by Nanak and made systematic by his successors, langar is theology made edible: equality you can taste. The Golden Temple's langar feeds tens of thousands daily, and seva — selfless service, from cooking to sweeping the floors — is itself worship. Initiated Khalsa Sikhs additionally maintain the Five Ks: kesh (uncut hair, usually kept under the turban), kangha (a wooden comb), kara (a steel bracelet), kachera (cotton undergarment), and kirpan (a small sword signifying the duty to protect the vulnerable).

## Sacred Texts

The Guru Granth Sahib is unique among world scriptures in several ways. It is not about the Gurus; it is their own compositions, set in musical modes (ragas), compiled by the Gurus themselves rather than by later followers. And it is deliberately interfaith: alongside the Sikh Gurus it canonizes the verses of Hindu bhakti saints like Kabir, Ravidas, and Namdev and Muslim Sufis like Sheikh Farid — the only major scripture that gives voice to writers from other religions as scripture. Since 1708 it has been the Guru, treated with the ceremony due a living sovereign teacher. The Dasam Granth, associated with Guru Gobind Singh, holds secondary and more debated status.

## Branches and Movements

Sikhism is notably unified compared with most traditions of its size — the mainstream Khalsa orthodoxy, institutionally anchored at the Akal Takht in Amritsar, has no real rival. Within and alongside it are movements rather than schisms: the Namdharis, who believe the line of living Gurus continued; the Nirankaris, originally a reform movement against ritualism; Sant lineages and the diaspora-born 3HO community of Western converts. The sharper divisions in modern Sikh history have been political rather than doctrinal, from the Singh Sabha reform movement of the colonial era through the trauma of Partition, which cut Punjab in two, and the conflicts of the 1980s.

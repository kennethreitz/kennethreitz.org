# Zoroastrianism

> *The religion the West forgot it remembered — heaven and hell, angels and a devil, judgment and resurrection, all tended for three thousand years by keepers of an unquenched flame.*

---

## Origins and History

Zoroastrianism is among the oldest continuously practiced religions on earth. Its prophet, Zarathushtra (Greek: Zoroaster), was a priest-poet of the Iranian steppe; scholars place him anywhere from around 1500 to 600 BCE, with the linguistic evidence of his hymns favoring the earlier range. Against the polytheism of his inherited religion, Zarathushtra proclaimed one supreme, uncreated God — Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord — and framed existence as a moral contest between truth (*asha*) and the lie (*druj*).

Under the Achaemenid, Parthian, and Sasanian empires, Zoroastrianism was the faith of the Persian world for over a millennium — the religion of Cyrus the Great, whose empire released the Jewish exiles from Babylon. The Arab conquest of Persia in the seventh century CE began a long decline under Islamic rule ([Islam](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/islam)); one community of the faithful sailed for India around the tenth century and became the Parsis, who prospered spectacularly in Bombay. Today the tradition numbers perhaps 100,000 to 125,000 people, concentrated in India and Iran with a growing diaspora — one of the world's smallest religions, and one of its most consequential.

---

## Core Beliefs

Ahura Mazda is wholly good, and evil is real but not His creation: it proceeds from Angra Mainyu (Ahriman), the destructive spirit, whose opposition to the good creation defines the present age. Whether this makes Zoroastrianism a strict dualism or a subtle monotheism — with evil destined for annihilation rather than eternal balance — has been debated inside and outside the tradition; classical theology is clear, at least, that the conflict ends. History is linear and purposeful. A savior figure, the Saoshyant, will come; the dead will be raised; a final ordeal of molten metal will purge the world; and evil will be destroyed forever in the renovation of existence (*frashokereti*).

Around Ahura Mazda stand the Amesha Spentas, the "Bounteous Immortals" — personified divine attributes such as Good Mind and Best Truth — and the yazatas, venerable beings often compared to archangels and angels. The individual soul faces judgment at the Chinvat Bridge, which widens for the righteous and narrows to a blade for the wicked, sending each to reward or punishment until the final restoration.

The resemblance to later Western religion is not coincidence. During and after the Persian period, Second-Temple [Judaism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/judaism) developed — in ways many scholars credit substantially to Iranian influence — its elaborated angelology, its cosmic adversary, and its hope of resurrection and last judgment. Those themes passed directly into the eschatology of [Christianity](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/christianity) and, later, [Islam](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/islam). The magi of Matthew's nativity are, by name, Zoroastrian priests. Few religions this small have echoed this loudly.

## Practices

Zoroastrian ethics compress into a famous triad: good thoughts, good words, good deeds (*humata, hukhta, hvarshta*). The tradition is world-affirming — asceticism is discouraged, and cultivating the earth, raising a family, and honest work are religious acts, since the material creation is Ahura Mazda's and joy in it is loyalty to its maker.

Fire is the great symbol — not worshipped, as outsiders long assumed, but venerated as the purest icon of divine light and truth. Fire temples house sacred flames in graded ranks; the Atash Behram at Udvada in India has burned continuously for centuries. The faithful pray five times daily, traditionally facing a source of light, and wear the *sudreh* (sacred shirt) and *kusti* (woven cord), tied and untied with prayers, received at the initiation ceremony called navjote. Purity laws run deep; the most famous funerary practice, exposure of the dead in *dakhmas* (towers of silence) so as to pollute neither earth nor fire, survives in parts of India, though burial and cremation are now common elsewhere. The year is punctuated by six seasonal festivals (*gahambars*) and Nowruz, the new year at the spring equinox, still celebrated far beyond the religion itself.

## Sacred Texts

The scripture is the Avesta, composed in an ancient Iranian language and transmitted orally for centuries before being written down under the Sasanians. Its oldest core is the seventeen Gathas — hymns attributed to Zarathushtra himself, dense, difficult, and addressed directly to Ahura Mazda — embedded in the liturgy called the Yasna. Around them gather the Yashts (hymns to individual yazatas), the Vendidad (purity law), and the Khordeh Avesta, the everyday prayer book. Much of the original canon was lost after the conquest; later Pahlavi (Middle Persian) works such as the Bundahishn and the Denkard preserve the cosmology and theology of the classical tradition.

## Branches and Communities

The living tradition has two historic centers. The Parsis of India, descendants of the medieval migration, built one of the subcontinent's most influential communities — the Tata industrial house, Freddie Mercury, and a long roll of jurists and philanthropists — while the Iranian Zoroastrians (Irani community, including those who remained in Yazd and Kerman) endured centuries as a taxed minority and survive today under the Islamic Republic. Doctrinal divisions are mild by the standards of larger faiths: a calendar dispute produced the Shahenshahi, Kadmi, and Fasli reckonings, and the sharpest modern controversy is whether one can convert in — traditionalist Parsis hold the community closed to those not born of Zoroastrian fathers, while reformists, especially in the diaspora, accept converts and intermarried families. With numbers falling, that question is existential.

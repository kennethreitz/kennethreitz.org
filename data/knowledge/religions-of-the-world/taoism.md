# Taoism

> *The Tao that can be told is not the eternal Tao. The name that can be named is not the eternal name.*

---

## Overview

Taoism (or Daoism) is China's native tradition of living in accord with the **Tao** — the Way — the unnameable source and pattern of everything that is. It is simultaneously a philosophy, an organized religion, a system of self-cultivation, and a sensibility: the intuition that the world already knows how to be the world, and that most human suffering comes from pushing against the grain instead of moving with it. Water is its favorite teacher — soft, yielding, and yet capable of carving canyons.

---

## Origins and History

Tradition credits the *Tao Te Ching* to **Laozi** (the "Old Master"), a possibly legendary archivist of the Zhou court said to have written its eighty-one brief chapters before vanishing westward through a mountain pass. The text itself likely crystallized in the 4th century BCE, alongside the *Zhuangzi* — a wilder, funnier book of parables in which its author dreams he is a butterfly and wakes unsure which is dreaming which. These two works anchor **philosophical Taoism** (*daojia*), which arose during the Warring States period as one answer among many to the question of how to live in a collapsing world. Where [Confucianism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/confucianism) answered with ritual and social duty, Taoism answered with retreat, spontaneity, and a deep suspicion of striving.

**Religious Taoism** (*daojiao*) emerged later, in the 2nd century CE, when Zhang Daoling founded the Way of the Celestial Masters and Taoism acquired priests, liturgies, a vast pantheon, and institutional form. Over the following centuries it absorbed and traded ideas with [Buddhism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/buddhism) as that tradition arrived from India — Chan (Zen) Buddhism is in many ways their child. Taoism flourished under some dynasties, suffered under others, was suppressed during the Cultural Revolution, and today lives on in mainland China, Taiwan, and the Chinese diaspora, both as organized religion and as the quiet philosophical current running underneath much of East Asian culture.

## Core Beliefs

The Tao is not a god. It does not command, judge, or answer prayers; it is the way things flow, prior to all names and categories. From it arise **yin and yang**, the complementary polarities whose interplay generates all change — dark and light, receptive and active, valley and mountain, each containing the seed of the other. Nothing in this scheme is evil; there is only balance and imbalance.

The central practical idea is **wu wei**, usually translated "non-action" but better understood as effortless action — acting without forcing, the way a skilled swimmer works with the current rather than thrashing against it. Its companion virtues are **ziran** (naturalness, self-so-ness) and **pu**, the "uncarved block": the simplicity of a thing before ambition and social polish have whittled it into something useful and false. The sage leads by not dominating, teaches without words, and accomplishes without claiming credit.

Chinese cosmology organizes change through the **wu xing** — the five phases of wood, fire, earth, metal, and water. These are not building blocks like the Western four elements (the vault's [Elements](/knowledge/elements) note covers that Greek lineage) but dynamic phases in a cycle, each generating and overcoming the others — a grammar of transformation that underlies Chinese medicine, feng shui, and internal alchemy alike.

There is a genuine resonance with [Stoicism](/knowledge/philosophy/stoicism) here — both traditions counsel alignment with the nature of things rather than rebellion against it — though the Stoic sage grips reason like a rudder while the Taoist sage lets go of the tiller entirely.

## Practices

Taoist practice aims at harmonizing the practitioner's **qi** (vital energy) with the larger patterns of the cosmos. This includes seated [Meditation](/knowledge/spirituality/meditation) and visualization, breathing disciplines, and the moving meditations of **taijiquan** (tai chi) and **qigong**, which circulate qi through the body's channels. **Internal alchemy** (*neidan*) refines the body's energies toward longevity and, in the tradition's boldest claims, immortality — the earlier external alchemy of elixirs having been abandoned after it poisoned a number of emperors.

Religious Taoism adds temple worship, offerings to deities and ancestors, communal festivals, and elaborate priestly liturgies of purification and renewal. Dietary regimens, ethical precepts, and feng shui — the siting of homes and graves in harmony with the land's energies — round out a tradition in which cosmology is never merely theoretical.

---

## Sacred Texts

The **Tao Te Ching** and the **Zhuangzi** remain the philosophical heart. Religious Taoism's canon, the **Daozang**, is enormous — roughly 1,400 texts of liturgy, alchemy, meditation manuals, and revelation accumulated over 1,500 years. The **I Ching** (Book of Changes), older than Taoism itself, is shared with the whole of Chinese thought but reads most naturally through a Taoist lens: sixty-four hexagrams mapping the permutations of yin and yang through time.

## Branches and Schools

| School | Character |
|--------|-----------|
| **Zhengyi** (Celestial Masters) | Oldest lineage; married priests, community ritual, dominant in Taiwan and southern China |
| **Quanzhen** (Complete Perfection) | Monastic, founded 12th century; meditation and internal alchemy, dominant in northern China |
| **Shangqing** (Highest Clarity) | Medieval visionary school centered on meditation and revealed scripture |
| **Lingbao** (Numinous Treasure) | Liturgical school that absorbed Buddhist ideas of universal salvation |

Philosophical Taoism, meanwhile, was never a membership organization at all — more a current of thought that any scholar, poet, or exhausted official could drink from, and most of them did.

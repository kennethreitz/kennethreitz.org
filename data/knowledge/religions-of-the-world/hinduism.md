# Hinduism

> *A river with many tributaries — the world's oldest living religious tradition, less a single faith than a civilization's ongoing conversation with the divine*

---

## Origins and History

Hinduism has no founder, no founding moment, and no single institution that speaks for it. The name itself is an outsider's word — "Hindu" originally just meant the people living beyond the Indus river. What the tradition calls itself is Sanātana Dharma, the eternal way, and its roots run deeper than recorded history. The earliest layer is the Vedic religion of the second millennium BCE, when Indo-Aryan peoples composed the Rigveda's hymns to gods of fire, storm, and dawn. Older still may be threads from the Indus Valley civilization, whose seals show figures seated in postures that look strikingly like [Yoga](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga).

Over roughly three thousand years the tradition transformed itself repeatedly without ever breaking continuity. The Upanishads (c. 800–300 BCE) turned ritual religion inward, asking what the self really is and answering with the equation of Atman and Brahman that [Vedanta](/knowledge/spirituality/vedanta) would later systematize. The great epics — the Mahabharata, containing the Bhagavad Gita, and the Ramayana — gave the philosophy flesh and narrative. Devotional (bhakti) movements from the medieval period onward democratized the tradition, insisting that love of God mattered more than caste or Sanskrit learning. Colonial encounter and figures like Swami Vivekananda carried Hindu thought to the West, where teachers like [Ram Dass](/knowledge/spirituality/ram-dass) continued the translation. Today it is the world's third-largest religion, with over a billion adherents, overwhelmingly in India and Nepal.

## Core Beliefs

Trying to state what all Hindus believe is famously hazardous — the tradition contains monists, dualists, theists, and near-atheists in good standing. But certain load-bearing concepts recur almost everywhere.

**Brahman and Atman.** Beneath the multiplicity of gods and worlds lies Brahman, the ultimate reality. The individual self, Atman, is in the Upanishadic view not separate from it — "Tat tvam asi," thou art That. How literally to take that identity is the central argument of Hindu philosophy, with [Vedanta](/knowledge/spirituality/vedanta)'s schools staking out every position from strict non-dualism to devotional dualism.

**Dharma.** Cosmic order and personal duty at once — the way things hold together, and one's role in holding them. Dharma varies by station, stage of life, and circumstance, which is why the Gita's crisis is precisely a dharma crisis: what does a warrior owe when every choice wounds?

**[Karma](/knowledge/spirituality/karma), samsara, and moksha.** Action bears fruit; consequences follow the actor across lifetimes through the cycle of rebirth (samsara). Liberation (moksha) is release from that cycle — not a place but a recognition, the Atman knowing itself. Hinduism treats moksha as the ultimate human aim, though it honors worldly aims (pleasure, prosperity, duty) as legitimate stations along the way.

**The gods.** Hinduism's relationship to its deities is subtle. [Hindu Mythology](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology) presents a vast pantheon — [Brahma](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/brahma) the creator, [Vishnu](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/vishnu) the preserver with his avatars including [Krishna](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/krishna), [Shiva](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/shiva) the transformer, the fierce mother [Kali](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/kali), beloved [Ganesha](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/ganesha) the remover of obstacles — yet many Hindus understand these as faces of one reality. The pattern is closer to [Henotheism](/knowledge/spirituality/henotheism) than either simple polytheism or exclusive monotheism: worship one god as supreme without denying the others.

## Practices

Hindu practice is extraordinarily varied, but its center of gravity is puja — ritual worship, whether at a home shrine with flowers, incense, and offered food, or at a temple where the deity's image (murti) is treated as an honored living presence. Pilgrimage matters: to Varanasi on the Ganges, to the great temple cities of the south, to the Kumbh Mela, which periodically becomes the largest human gathering on earth. Festivals like Diwali and Holi structure the year with light and color.

Alongside the devotional stream runs the contemplative one. [Yoga](/knowledge/spirituality/yoga) in its classical sense is a complete discipline for stilling the mind, of which the postures the West knows are one limb among eight. [Meditation](/knowledge/spirituality/meditation), mantra repetition, breath work, and the subtle-body map of the [Chakras](/knowledge/chakras) all belong to this interior technology. The Gita famously organizes the paths as karma yoga (action without attachment), bhakti yoga (devotion), jnana yoga (knowledge), and raja yoga (meditation) — different doors suited to different temperaments, opening on the same room.

## Sacred Texts

Hindu scripture divides into shruti ("heard" — revealed) and smriti ("remembered" — tradition). The four Vedas and their culminating Upanishads are shruti, the tradition's bedrock. The smriti layer is where most Hindus actually live: the epics, the Puranas with their mythological cycles, and above all the Bhagavad Gita, which compresses the tradition's philosophy into Krishna's counsel to a paralyzed warrior and has served for centuries as Hinduism's most portable scripture.

## Major Branches and Schools

| Tradition | Focus |
|-----------|-------|
| Vaishnavism | [Vishnu](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/vishnu) and his avatars, especially [Krishna](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/krishna) and Rama, as supreme |
| Shaivism | [Shiva](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/shiva) as supreme reality; strong ascetic and tantric currents |
| Shaktism | The Goddess (Devi/Shakti, including [Kali](/knowledge/mythology/hindu-mythology/kali)) as ultimate power |
| Smartism | Worship of five deities as equivalent forms; aligned with Advaita [Vedanta](/knowledge/spirituality/vedanta) |

Philosophically, six classical schools (darshanas) developed, of which [Vedanta](/knowledge/spirituality/vedanta) became the most influential — itself split among Shankara's non-dualism, Ramanuja's qualified non-dualism, and Madhva's dualism. [Buddhism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/buddhism), [Jainism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/jainism), and [Sikhism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/sikhism) all arose on Indian soil in conversation with — and often in argument with — these Hindu frameworks, sharing the vocabulary of karma and liberation while contesting its grammar.

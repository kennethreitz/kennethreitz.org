# Bahá'í Faith

> *The earth is but one country, and mankind its citizens.*

---

## Overview

The Bahá'í Faith is the youngest of the world's independent religions — founded in 19th-century Persia by **Bahá'u'lláh**, whom Bahá'ís regard as the latest in the long line of divine messengers that includes Abraham, Moses, Zoroaster, Krishna, the Buddha, Jesus, and Muhammad. Its central conviction is threefold: the oneness of God, the oneness of religion, and the oneness of humanity. Roughly five to eight million Bahá'ís live in virtually every country on earth, making it one of the most geographically widespread religions in existence despite its youth and size.

---

## Origins and History

The story begins in 1844 in Shiraz, when a young merchant, **the Báb** ("the Gate"), declared himself the bearer of a new revelation and the herald of one still greater to come — "Him Whom God shall make manifest." The Bábí movement spread rapidly through Persia and was met with ferocious persecution; thousands of followers were killed, and the Báb himself was executed by firing squad in Tabriz in 1850.

Among his followers was a nobleman, Mírzá Ḥusayn-'Alí, who took the title **Bahá'u'lláh** ("Glory of God"). Imprisoned in 1852 in the Síyáh-Chál, a notorious Tehran dungeon, he experienced the intimation of his mission there — and in 1863, in a garden outside Baghdad, declared himself the one the Báb had foretold. The rest of his life was a sequence of banishments at Ottoman hands: Baghdad, Constantinople, Adrianople, and finally the prison city of Akka in Palestine, where he died in 1892. That his faith's world center sits today in Haifa and Akka is an accident of empire — the location of his exile, not his choosing.

Leadership passed by explicit written covenant, a point of pride in a tradition acutely aware of how other faiths fractured over succession: first to his son **'Abdu'l-Bahá**, who carried the faith to Europe and North America; then to his great-grandson **Shoghi Effendi**, the Guardian, who built its administrative order; and since 1963 to the **Universal House of Justice**, an elected nine-member council. The community's heaviest ongoing burden is persecution in Iran, where Bahá'ís remain the largest non-Muslim religious minority and are systematically denied education, employment, and legal protection.

## Core Beliefs

The keystone doctrine is **progressive revelation**. God is one, unknowable in essence, and educates humanity through a succession of **Manifestations of God** — mirrors reflecting the same divine light to different ages. On this view [Judaism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/judaism), [Christianity](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/christianity), [Islam](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/islam), [Zoroastrianism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/zoroastrianism), [Hinduism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/hinduism), and [Buddhism](/knowledge/religions-of-the-world/buddhism) are not rival claims but chapters of a single unfolding book, each revelation suited to its era's capacity, and none of them final. Bahá'u'lláh is the chapter for this age; Bahá'ís expect further Manifestations in the future, though not for at least a thousand years. Religious truth, in this frame, is relative to its moment — the differences between faiths reflect the needs of their times, not contradictions in God.

From the oneness of humanity flow the faith's distinctive social teachings, radical for 19th-century Persia and still pointed today: the equality of women and men, the elimination of all prejudice, the harmony of science and religion (a truth that contradicts reason, Bahá'ís hold, should be reexamined), universal education, the abolition of extremes of wealth and poverty, an auxiliary universal language, and the eventual federation of the world's peoples. The independent investigation of truth is an obligation — no clergy stands between the believer and the text. The soul is immortal, and heaven and hell are read as conditions of nearness to or remoteness from God rather than places.

## Practices

Bahá'í practice is austere in ritual and rich in rhythm. Individual life is anchored by daily **obligatory prayer**, reading of scripture morning and evening, and an annual nineteen-day sunrise-to-sunset **fast** in early March. The Bahá'í calendar runs nineteen months of nineteen days; each month opens with the **Nineteen Day Feast**, the community's devotional, consultative, and social gathering. There is no clergy, no sermon, and almost no liturgy — affairs are governed by elected councils (Local and National Spiritual Assemblies) using **consultation**, a distinctive discipline of frank, detached group decision-making in which ideas, once offered, belong to the group rather than their proposer.

Alcohol and partisan politics are both abstained from; work performed in a spirit of service is itself ranked as worship. Community life increasingly centers on open study circles, children's classes, and devotional gatherings that welcome anyone. The faith's houses of worship — nine-sided temples open to people of every religion or none, from Wilmette to New Delhi's Lotus Temple — enact the theology in architecture: many doors, one dome.

## Sacred Texts

Bahá'u'lláh wrote voluminously — some 15,000 tablets survive — and Bahá'í scripture is unusual among world religions in consisting largely of the founder's own authenticated writings rather than later recollection. The **Kitáb-i-Aqdas** (Most Holy Book) is the book of laws; the **Kitáb-i-Íqán** (Book of Certitude) the chief doctrinal work, setting out progressive revelation; the **Hidden Words** a collection of brief ethical and mystical aphorisms; the **Seven Valleys** a Sufi-inflected map of the soul's journey. The writings of the Báb and of 'Abdu'l-Bahá are also scripture; those of Shoghi Effendi and the Universal House of Justice are authoritative interpretation and legislation but not revelation.

---

## Branches

There essentially are none — a rarity worth noting. The explicit written covenant of succession has kept the overwhelming body of Bahá'ís in a single worldwide community; the handful of schismatic groups that have appeared over succession disputes number at most a few thousand members combined. For a religion whose founding message is unity, the fact functions as a kind of standing argument.

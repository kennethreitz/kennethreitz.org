# Existentialism

> *Existence precedes essence - freedom, authenticity, and meaning*

---

## Core Tenets

### Existence Precedes Essence
We exist first, then define ourselves through our choices. There is no pre-determined human nature. We create our own meaning, and with that comes radical freedom and radical responsibility.

### Freedom and Responsibility
We are "condemned to be free" -- there is no escaping choice. We are responsible for every decision we make, and claiming otherwise is bad faith.

### Authenticity
Being true to yourself means refusing to live by others' scripts, facing existential realities head-on, and owning your choices fully.

### Absurdity
The universe has no inherent meaning, yet we seek meaning in it anyway. This is the fundamental tension of human existence -- and we must create meaning despite it.

---

## Key Philosophers

### Soren Kierkegaard (1813-1855)
**"Father of Existentialism"**

Kierkegaard championed the leap of faith and subjective truth. He mapped the terrain of anxiety and despair, proposed three stages of existence (Aesthetic, Ethical, Religious), and insisted on the individual against the crowd.

### Friedrich Nietzsche (1844-1900)
**"God is Dead"**

Nietzsche confronted the death of God and the meaning crisis it left behind. He answered with the will to power, the vision of the Ubermensch, eternal recurrence, and the imperative to create your own values. Amor fati -- love of fate.

### Martin Heidegger (1889-1976)
**Being and Time**

Heidegger introduced being-toward-death, the distinction between authenticity and "the They," thrownness and facticity, care as the fundamental structure of existence, and Dasein (being-there) as the ground of all inquiry.

### Jean-Paul Sartre (1905-1980)
**"Existence precedes essence"**

Sartre insisted on radical freedom, exposed bad faith as self-deception, declared "hell is other people," and placed nothingness at the heart of consciousness. Engagement and responsibility follow -- we are condemned to be free.

### Albert Camus (1913-1960)
**The Absurd Hero**

Camus named the Absurd condition and offered Sisyphus as its metaphor. His answer: revolt, freedom, passion. "One must imagine Sisyphus happy." Embrace absurdity without surrendering to suicide or god.

### Simone de Beauvoir (1908-1986)
**Feminist Existentialism**

"One is not born, but becomes a woman." De Beauvoir brought existentialism to bear on gender, developing an ethics of ambiguity that addressed freedom and oppression together.

## Key Concepts

### Angst/Anxiety
Existential anxiety is the awareness of freedom itself -- the dizziness of possibility, the confrontation with nothingness. It is not pathological but ontological, woven into the fabric of being human.

### Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi)
Self-deception in the existential sense means denying your own freedom, hiding from responsibility, living inauthentically. "I had no choice" is the quintessential lie of bad faith.

### The Absurd
The gap between the human need for meaning and the universe's silence. This fundamental tension cannot be resolved, only lived. Camus answers it with revolt against absurdity.

### Thrownness
We are thrown into existence without choosing our birth, family, or era. This facticity must be accepted -- and then we choose what to do with it.

### Being-Toward-Death
Death is the ultimate possibility. Awareness of finitude, far from paralyzing, opens the door to authenticity. Being-toward-death becomes a way of life.

### The Other
Sartre wrote, "Hell is other people." The gaze of others objectifies, and intersubjectivity produces both recognition and alienation.

---

## Existential Themes

### Meaning and Meaninglessness
No cosmic purpose is given. We must create our own meaning -- absurd but necessary. The question is whether that meaning is authentic or merely imposed.

### Freedom and Limitation
Consciousness is absolutely free, yet constrained by facticity. Freedom operates within situation. You cannot not choose.

### Alienation and Authenticity
Modern life alienates. "The They" (das Man) enforces conformity. Authentic existence is rare and requires courage.

### Death and Finitude
Death is the defining limit that gives urgency to life. It is both the source of anxiety and the path to authenticity.

---

## Existentialism in Practice

### Living Existentially

**Acknowledge Freedom.** You always have choice. Stop saying "I have to." Own your decisions and accept responsibility.

**Face Anxiety.** Anxiety reveals freedom. Don't flee into distraction -- sit with uncertainty. That's existential courage.

**Create Meaning.** Don't wait for cosmic purpose. Decide what matters, commit to projects, engage authentically.

**Accept Absurdity.** The universe doesn't care. Meaning is human creation. Rebel against meaninglessness. Imagine Sisyphus happy.

**Be Authentic.** Know yourself. Don't live others' values. Face your mortality. Make conscious choices.

---

## Existential Anxiety vs Clinical Anxiety

### Existential Anxiety
Ontological -- part of being human. It is the response to freedom, the awareness of nothingness. It cannot be "cured." It must be embraced.

### Clinical Anxiety
A pathological condition that interferes with function. It can be treated and is categorically different from existential anxiety.

**Relationship:** Clinical anxiety may mask existential anxiety, and existential awareness can trigger clinical symptoms. Both are valid, operating at different levels.

## Criticisms

### "Too Pessimistic"
The response: existentialism is realistic about the human condition. It acknowledges suffering but also affirms life. As Camus put it, "One must imagine Sisyphus happy."

### "Nihilistic"
The opposite is true. Nihilism says nothing matters. Existentialism says we create what matters -- affirmation despite absurdity.

### "Too Individualistic"
A valid concern. Later existentialists addressed it -- Sartre turned to Marxism, de Beauvoir wrote on oppression. Freedom is intersubjective.

### "Elitist"
This critique has teeth. Not everyone can afford an existential crisis. Existentialism assumes the privilege to philosophize and must reckon with material conditions.

---

## Existentialism and Other Philosophies

### vs Essentialism
Essentialism places essence before existence -- Plato's pre-determined nature. Existentialism rejects this entirely. We define ourselves.

### and Phenomenology
Husserl's method of going "back to the things themselves," bracketing assumptions to describe experience directly, became the methodological foundation for Heidegger and Sartre.

### and Absurdism
Camus insisted absurdism was not existentialism. Both confront meaninglessness, but absurdism says accept the absurdity without creating meaning, while existentialism says create meaning despite it. A subtle but important difference.

---

## Modern Relevance

### Contemporary Life
The secular age faces a meaning crisis. Freedom produces paralysis through too many choices. Authenticity struggles against social media performance. Anxiety has become epidemic, and death denial pervades the culture.

### Existential Therapy
Irvin Yalom and Viktor Frankl built therapeutic practices around confronting the ultimate concerns: death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness. The aim is creating an authentic life. Frankl's logotherapy finds healing through meaning.

### Cultural Impact
Existentialism runs through literature (Dostoevsky, Kafka, Beckett) and film (Bergman, Tarkovsky). The philosophy remains vibrant because it speaks directly to modern alienation.

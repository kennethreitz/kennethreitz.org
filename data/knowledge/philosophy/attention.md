# Attention

> *Where your attention goes, everything follows — design, consciousness, money, love.*

---

## The Fundamental Currency

Attention is the thing underneath everything else. Before money, before time, before energy — attention. It is what you spend when you look at something, listen to something, care about something. It is the raw material of consciousness itself, the beam of awareness that turns undifferentiated experience into meaning. Every design decision is a decision about where attention goes. Every economic transaction is an exchange of attention. Every relationship is an agreement about mutual attention. Every spiritual practice is a discipline of attention.

Kenneth's work converges on this insight from multiple directions at once — as a designer, as a consciousness researcher, as someone living with a mind that doesn't always get to choose where attention goes. Attention is where all of it meets.

---

## Attention as Design

[For Humans](/knowledge/philosophy/for-humans) is a design philosophy, and at its core, design is the art of directing attention. A well-designed interface guides your eye to what matters. A well-designed API guides your cognition to the most natural way of accomplishing a task. A well-designed anything reduces the friction between intention and action by placing attention where it needs to be.

[Visual Hierarchy and the Shape of Attention](/essays/2025-09-19-visual-hierarchy-and-the-shape-of-attention) explores this as formal principle. Visual hierarchy is the designer's technology for controlling the sequence and priority of attention — what you see first, what you see second, what you see last. Size, contrast, color, spacing, position — all of these are tools for shaping the path that attention takes through a composition. Good design feels effortless because attention flows naturally. Bad design feels confusing because attention has nowhere to land.

But design doesn't just direct attention. It *costs* attention. Every element on a screen, every option in an interface, every word in a document demands a micro-expenditure of the user's attentional budget. This is why Kenneth's design philosophy gravitates toward simplicity — not because complexity is inherently wrong, but because unnecessary complexity wastes the most precious resource your user has. Respecting attention is the foundation of designing for humans.

## Attention as Spiritual Practice

[The Gift of Attention](/essays/2025-09-06-the_gift_of_attention) reframes attention from cognitive resource to sacred offering. To attend to something fully — to give it your complete, undivided presence — is an act of love. This is what every contemplative tradition teaches at its core. [Meditation](/knowledge/spirituality/meditation) is the practice of directing attention deliberately and holding it there. Prayer is attention offered to the divine. Deep listening is attention offered to another person. The quality of your attention determines the quality of your experience, your relationships, and your life.

For Kenneth, this is not abstract spirituality. It is daily practice complicated by the clinical reality of schizoaffective disorder. When perception becomes unreliable, attention takes on a different urgency — attending to what's real versus what's generated by a mind under pressure. The gift of attention becomes also the discipline of attention, the discernment of attention, the sometimes-terrifying responsibility of choosing what to attend to when your own mind is offering multiple competing realities.

## Attention Under Siege

The Algorithm Eats series documents the industrial theft of attention. Every engagement-optimized platform is an attention extraction machine — harvesting human presence, converting it into engagement metrics, selling it to advertisers. The infinite scroll, the autoplay, the notification badge, the variable reward schedule — these are technologies designed to capture attention against the user's own interests.

[Virtue Inversion](/knowledge/philosophy/virtue-inversion) operates through captured attention. You cannot invert someone's values unless you first control what they pay attention to. The algorithm doesn't argue against wisdom — it simply directs your attention toward impulsive content until wisdom has no airtime. It doesn't argue against temperance — it just fills your attention so completely with stimulation that moderation never gets a word in. The theft of attention is upstream of everything else the algorithm eats.

This creates a strange loop: the same capacity that makes humans conscious — our ability to attend — is the capacity being exploited. Attention is both the highest gift we can offer and the most valuable commodity that can be extracted from us. The difference between gift and extraction is consent, intention, and who benefits.

## Attention and the Mind That Struggles

For someone living with schizoaffective disorder, attention is not an unlimited resource freely allocated by conscious choice. The clinical reality includes attentional flooding — too much input, too much salience, too many patterns demanding attention at once. It includes attentional capture — attention seized by perceptual experiences that may or may not correspond to external reality. It includes the exhaustion of sustained attention as a finite resource depleted faster by a mind working harder than baseline to maintain consensus perception.

This lived experience informs Kenneth's understanding of attention in ways that theory alone never could. When you cannot always control where your attention goes, you develop a deeper appreciation for what attention actually is, what it costs, and what it means to give it deliberately. The design philosophy, the spiritual practice, and the clinical reality are not separate domains. They are the same insight — attention is the fundamental currency — experienced from different vantage points of the same life.

---

## Where They Meet

The convergence is this: Kenneth designs interfaces that respect attention because he knows what it costs. He writes about attention as spiritual practice because he knows what it's worth. He documents the algorithmic theft of attention because he knows what's being stolen. And he lives with a condition that makes attention's fragility impossible to ignore. The concept of attention sits at the exact center of his work — the point where the designer, the mystic, the researcher, and the patient all look at the same thing from different angles and see the same truth.

Attention is all you have. Spend it wisely. Guard it fiercely. Give it generously. And notice — always notice — who is trying to take it from you without asking.

# Pride

> *The first sin was not wanting to be bad. It was wanting to be the source.*

---

## The Tradition

Evagrius listed pride (*hyperephania*) and vainglory (*kenodoxia*) as two separate thoughts — the craving for others' admiration and the deeper conviction of one's own self-sufficiency. Gregory the Great fused them and then did something more radical: he lifted pride out of the list entirely and named it the root from which the other seven grow, "the queen of sins." Later tradition folded it back in as the first and gravest of the seven. Either way, the diagnosis is the same — pride is not one fault among others but the posture that makes every other fault possible.

## The Disordered Desire

Beneath the arrogance is something almost sympathetic: the desire to matter, bent into the refusal to depend. Pride is the excellence-hunger that cannot tolerate a source outside itself — it must be self-made, self-justified, beyond correction and beyond debt. The behaviors vary wildly, from strutting to the subtler pride that performs humility for an audience. What they share is the underlying claim: *I am not contingent.* Which is why the tradition considered it the direct inversion of the creature's actual position, and why it isolates so completely — pride can be admired, but it cannot be met.

## In Purgatorio

Dante puts pride on the first terrace, lowest on the mountain because it is the heaviest to carry — love of self perverted into looking down on the neighbor. The penitents walk bent double under enormous stones, forced at last to look at the ground, while the pavement beneath them is carved with images of pride brought low and the cliff face with exempla of humility, the Annunciation first among them. Dante confesses this is the terrace where he expects to do his own time.

## The Remedy

The counter-practice is [Humility](/knowledge/virtues-and-vices/virtues/humility) — not self-erasure but accurate self-assessment, the willingness to be contingent, corrected, and indebted. The stone on the back teaches what the neck would not learn: how to bow.

## The Enneagram Passion

In the [Enneagram](/knowledge/enneagram), pride is the passion of [Type 2](/knowledge/enneagram/type-2-the-helper) — a pride that hides inside generosity. The Two gives tirelessly while quietly refusing to have needs of its own: *others* need help; the Two is above needing. It is the desert's diagnosis in a warmer costume — self-sufficiency dressed as service — and a reminder that pride's favorite disguise has always been virtue.

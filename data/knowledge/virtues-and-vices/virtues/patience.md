# Patience

> *Patience is not waiting. It is who you remain while you wait.*

---

## Where It Belongs

Patience stands among the seven heavenly virtues as the remedy for [Wrath](/knowledge/virtues-and-vices/seven-deadly-sins/wrath). Its credentials are old: Tertullian and Cyprian each wrote a treatise *On Patience* before the sin lists were even settled, and Prudentius's *Psychomachia* — the fifth-century battle-poem behind the whole remedial scheme — gives Patientia one of the poem's best scenes: she defeats Wrath without striking a blow, standing calmly in her armor until Anger, exhausted by its own fury, destroys itself. The image is the doctrine.

## What It Is

The Latin root is *pati*, to suffer, to undergo — patience is the capacity to bear a wrong, a delay, or a discomfort without being deformed by it. The KJV's word is better: *longsuffering* — suffering that stays long without curdling into retaliation or collapse. It is not passivity; the patient person still sees the injustice clearly, still acts when action serves. What patience declines is the demand for *immediate* repayment, the seizure of the judge's bench. That refusal keeps the eye of judgment clear — which is exactly what wrath's smoke obscures — so that when the patient person finally moves, the movement is justice rather than revenge.

## The Sin It Answers

Patience answers [Wrath](/knowledge/virtues-and-vices/seven-deadly-sins/wrath) — the love of justice bent into the love of retribution. On Dante's third terrace the angry walk blind in black smoke while visions of patience arrive directly in the mind: Mary answering her lost son with a question instead of a rebuke, Stephen forgiving his killers on his knees. The remedy is not the suppression of anger but its outlasting — Prudentius's insight, that fury spends itself against a person who declines to become it.

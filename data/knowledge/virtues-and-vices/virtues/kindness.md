# Kindness

> *Kindness is charity at street level — love with no occasion grander than Tuesday.*

---

## Where It Belongs

Kindness holds a place among the seven heavenly virtues as the remedy for [Envy](/knowledge/virtues-and-vices/seven-deadly-sins/envy). The medieval lists name it variously — *humanitas*, *benevolentia*, sometimes brotherly love — and it can look like the modest one on the list, the virtue nobody writes epics about. That modesty is deceptive. Paul puts it in the center of his portrait of charity ("charity suffereth long, and is kind"), and the Galatians harvest includes it under *gentleness* — see Fruits of the Spirit. The tradition understood that the smallest virtue on the list is the one practiced most often.

## What It Is

Kindness is active goodwill — not the feeling of warmth but the *work* of the neighbor's good, done in the ordinary grain of a day. The English word carries its own theology: kindness treats the other as *kin*, as one's own kind. That derivation is the whole doctrine. Where envy looks at the neighbor and sees a rival whose fullness proves my lack, kindness looks at the same person and sees family — someone whose flourishing enlarges rather than indicts me. It requires no heroism, no occasion, and no reciprocity; it is charity broken into small enough denominations to spend hourly.

## The Sin It Answers

Kindness answers [Envy](/knowledge/virtues-and-vices/seven-deadly-sins/envy) — sorrow at another's good. The remedy is behavioral, not argumentative: envy is a way of seeing, and it is retrained by acting against it, one unforced act of goodwill at a time, until the neighbor's good stops registering as theft. On Dante's second terrace the envious, eyes sewn shut, learn to lean on one another — held up by exactly the people they once looked against. Practiced kindness is that lesson taken voluntarily.

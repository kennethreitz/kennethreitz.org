# Chastity (virtue)

> *Chastity is not the absence of desire. It is desire that has learned to see.*

---

## Where It Belongs

Chastity (*castitas*) stands among the seven heavenly virtues as the remedy for [Lust](/knowledge/virtues-and-vices/seven-deadly-sins/lust). In Prudentius's *Psychomachia* she is Pudicitia, the second combatant to take the field, cutting down Libido with a sword — an image the Middle Ages loved and modernity finds easy to mock, which says something about both eras. The virtue has suffered more shrinkage than any other on the list: reduced in the popular mind to mere abstinence, a fence around an absence. The tradition meant something more interesting.

## What It Is

Chastity is the integration of eros — sexual desire brought into alignment with love, commitment, and the actual personhood of the other, so that wanting someone and seeing them become the same act. The root, *castus*, means pure in the sense of *unmixed*: desire unadulterated by use, by fantasy that replaces the person, by the reduction of another to an instrument of sensation. It is therefore a virtue for the married as much as the celibate — its opposite is not sex but *consumption*. The chaste person has not amputated desire; they have unified it, which is why the tradition ranked chastity a form of temperance rather than of fear.

## The Sin It Answers

Chastity answers [Lust](/knowledge/virtues-and-vices/seven-deadly-sins/lust) — the longing for union bent into the use of persons. Dante's image remains the best in the literature: on the seventh terrace, the last before Eden, the lustful walk inside living fire that refines rather than destroys, and desire emerges not smaller but clear-sighted. The remedy for the counterfeit of love is not less fire. It is fire that illuminates what it touches.
